What a Free Website Audit Actually Checks (And Why It Matters Before You Run Ads)

"Free website audit" sounds like a lead-magnet gimmick, but a real one, built on Google's own Lighthouse engine, is checking the exact technical signals that decide whether traffic converts or bounces. Here's what actually gets scored, and why it's worth running before, not after, you start paying for clicks.

K
Kryvex Media Team
Oct 17, 2026 · 10 min read

A real website audit isn't a sales pitch dressed up as a tool. When it's built on a legitimate engine (the free instant audit on this site, like most credible ones, runs on Google's own open-source Lighthouse engine), it's running the same automated checks Google itself uses internally to understand how a page performs for a real visitor. That distinction matters, because the results aren't an opinion about your website, they're a measurement against published, public thresholds. Knowing what's actually being measured, and why each piece matters, turns a 30-second score from a curiosity into something you can act on, especially before you start paying to send traffic to that same page.

Performance: the score that affects everything else

Performance is usually the headline number, and it's built around Google's Core Web Vitals, a published, documented set of three metrics Google uses to evaluate real-world page experience:

  • Largest Contentful Paint (LCP) — how long it takes for the biggest visible element on the page (usually a hero image or headline) to fully render. Google's own published threshold for "good" is 2.5 seconds or less.
  • Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS) — whether elements jump around while the page is still loading, like a button shifting down right as someone tries to tap it. Google's "good" threshold is a score of 0.1 or lower.
  • Interaction to Next Paint (INP) — how responsive the page feels when someone actually clicks or taps something. The published "good" threshold is 200 milliseconds or less.

These three numbers aren't arbitrary. Google has stated directly that Core Web Vitals are a factor in how pages are ranked, and separately, they're a direct proxy for whether a real visitor sticks around or leaves. An audit that returns a low performance score isn't being pedantic about milliseconds, it's flagging that a meaningful share of visitors are experiencing a slow or jumpy page, which is a conversion problem whether or not it's also an SEO problem.

SEO fundamentals: the basics that get missed constantly

The SEO section of a real audit isn't checking keyword strategy, that requires human judgment an automated tool can't make. It's checking the mechanical basics that are genuinely easy to get wrong: whether the page has a unique, descriptive title tag, whether a meta description exists at all, whether the page is actually mobile-friendly (not just "doesn't look broken," but passes real viewport and tap-target checks), and whether anything in the page's own code is accidentally blocking it from being crawled or indexed, like a stray noindex tag left over from a staging environment. These sound basic, and they are, which is exactly why they're worth checking: a technically excellent SEO strategy built on top of a page that's accidentally set to noindex, or missing a title tag entirely, never gets the chance to work.

Mobile experience: not optional anymore

Mobile usability gets checked as its own category because it's no longer a secondary concern layered on top of a desktop site, Google has used mobile-first indexing for years, meaning the mobile version of a page is generally what gets crawled and evaluated first, not the desktop version. The audit checks things like whether text is readable without the visitor needing to zoom, whether tap targets (buttons, links) are large enough and spaced far enough apart to tap accurately on a touchscreen, and whether content fits the viewport without horizontal scrolling. A page that looks polished on a 27-inch monitor and is cramped or broken on a phone is, for most businesses today, a page that's broken for the majority of actual visitors.

Accessibility and best practices: the quieter categories

Two more categories round out a full Lighthouse-based audit, and they're worth understanding even though they get less attention than speed and SEO:

  • Accessibility checks things like whether images have descriptive alt text, whether color contrast is high enough to be readable, and whether the page can be navigated without a mouse. These checks matter for real visitors using screen readers or keyboard navigation, and a low score here means a real segment of potential customers can't actually use the site.
  • Best practices covers technical hygiene: whether the site is served over HTTPS, whether there are JavaScript errors showing up in the browser console, and whether images are served in reasonably modern, efficient formats. None of these are glamorous, but each one is a real, fixable signal, not noise.

A free audit is a fast, objective technical snapshot, not a full strategic review. It can tell you your LCP is 4.8 seconds; it can't tell you which competitor is outranking you for your highest-value keyword, or whether your backlink profile has a spam problem. Both kinds of review matter, they just answer different questions.

Why this matters specifically before you spend on ads

A paid ad only buys the click. What happens after the click is entirely determined by the page the visitor lands on, and that's exactly the layer an audit measures. If the landing page has a slow LCP, a visitor who clicked an ad they were genuinely interested in can leave before the page is even fully rendered, and that's a lost conversion the ad targeting had nothing to do with. If the mobile experience is cramped on the exact device most of the paid traffic is arriving on, the same thing happens. This is why a campaign that looks "broken" on paper (high cost per click, low conversion rate) sometimes isn't a targeting or budget problem at all, it's a landing-page problem that no amount of ad optimization will fix on its own. Running a free audit on the actual landing page before launching, or spot-checking it on an existing underperforming campaign, is a five-minute step that can explain a result weeks of bidding adjustments couldn't.

What to actually do with the results

A score by itself isn't an action plan, it's a starting point for triage. A practical way to use it:

  • Treat performance issues as the priority if the score is low. A slow LCP or a high CLS affects every single visitor on every single page, paid or organic, so fixing it has the broadest impact per hour spent.
  • Treat SEO and mobile issues as blockers, not nice-to-haves. A missing title tag or a broken mobile layout isn't a minor polish item, it's actively preventing the page from working as intended for a share of visitors.
  • Re-run the audit after fixing anything significant. It takes 30 seconds and confirms the fix actually moved the number, rather than assuming it did.
  • Don't chase a perfect 100 across every category. Diminishing returns set in fast past a certain point, and the time is usually better spent on the next real conversion or content issue than shaving off the last few points on a score.

If the honest answer to "why isn't this page converting" turns out to be a technical one rather than a targeting one, that's a cheaper problem to have found early. Frequently asked questions

What does a free website audit actually check?

A real automated audit (the kind built on Google's Lighthouse engine) checks four main areas: performance (how fast the page loads and becomes usable, measured against Core Web Vitals), SEO basics (title tags, meta descriptions, mobile-friendliness, crawlability signals), accessibility (whether the page can be used by people relying on screen readers or keyboard navigation), and best practices (HTTPS, console errors, and other technical hygiene checks).

Is a free website audit the same as a full SEO audit?

No. A free automated audit gives you a fast, objective technical snapshot in under a minute, which is genuinely useful but limited to what a script can measure. A full SEO audit adds things no automated tool can judge on its own: competitor positioning, keyword gap analysis, backlink quality, and content strategy. The free audit is the right first step, not a replacement for a full strategic review.

Why should I run an audit before spending money on ads?

Paid ads only pay for the click, not the result. If the page a visitor lands on is slow to load or hard to use on a phone, a meaningful share of that paid traffic leaves before the page even finishes loading, regardless of how well the ad itself was targeted. Fixing the landing page first means the same ad budget converts more of the traffic it already paid for.
