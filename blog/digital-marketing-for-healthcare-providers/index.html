Digital Marketing for Healthcare Providers: What HIPAA Actually Allows

Every industry worries about brand reputation and ad compliance. Healthcare is the one industry where getting marketing wrong can trigger a federal privacy investigation. Review responses, tracking pixels and ad platforms all carry real legal exposure that a restaurant or a retailer never has to think about — and real enforcement actions show exactly where practices have crossed the line. Here's what the rules actually say, and what a compliant marketing system looks like underneath them.

K
Kryvex Media Team
Oct 23, 2026 · 11 min read

Most marketing advice is industry-agnostic once you strip away the branding. Build a complete Google Business Profile, run a review strategy, run targeted ads, write content that answers real search intent — the mechanics barely change between a law firm and a landscaper. Healthcare is the real exception. A clinic, dental practice or wellness brand is operating under HIPAA, under FTC rules that have gotten noticeably stricter in the last two years, and under ad-platform policies written specifically because this category gets exploited. None of that means healthcare providers should market less aggressively than anyone else — plenty of real patient demand is one search away from finding a different provider instead. It means the "how" has real legal weight attached to it that most marketing guides skip entirely.

The review-response trap: why the obvious reply can trigger a federal investigation

Responding to a bad online review feels instinctive — defend the practice, correct the record, show prospective patients the full story. For a healthcare provider, that instinct is exactly how real HIPAA Privacy Rule violations happen. Legal commentary summarizing HHS Office for Civil Rights (OCR) enforcement is consistent on one specific point: HIPAA does not permit a covered provider to confirm, in a public reply, that the reviewer was even a patient at all — not even when the reviewer has already said so themselves in their own review. Confirming a treatment relationship is itself protected health information (PHI), separate from whatever clinical detail might come after it.

This isn't theoretical. On June 5, 2023, HHS announced a resolution agreement with Manasa Health Center, a psychiatric practice in New Jersey, after a patient complained in April 2020 that the practice had disclosed their PHI while replying to a negative online review. OCR's investigation then found three more instances of the same thing — the practice had disclosed PHI in responses to four different patients' negative reviews in total. Manasa Health Center paid $30,000 and entered a two-year, OCR-monitored corrective action plan requiring new HIPAA policies, workforce training, and formal breach notifications sent to every patient whose information had been disclosed. HHS did not require an admission of wrongdoing, but the settlement itself — and HHS's own public announcement of it — is the clearest real demonstration of where the line sits and what crossing it costs.

A safe review response is boring on purpose: thank the reviewer for their feedback, say nothing that confirms or denies a treatment relationship, and invite them to call the office directly to discuss. That's the entire template. Anything more specific — a name, a visit date, a diagnosis, a correction to "what actually happened" — is the exact pattern OCR has already taken enforcement action over.

Google Business Profile still runs on the same three factors — with less room for error

The underlying mechanics of local search haven't changed for healthcare. Google's own Business Profile help documentation describes every local ranking as built on relevance, distance and prominence, with no way to pay for a better position — the same three factors that apply to a restaurant or a law firm. What's different for a medical or dental practice is how much of "prominence" gets built through reviews specifically, and how much more carefully those reviews have to be managed once they're flowing in. A complete, accurately categorized profile — correct specialties, real office hours, a steady and genuine stream of patient reviews, and a pre-written, legally safe response process for the negative ones — is free, entirely within a practice's control, and the first thing worth getting right, exactly as our local SEO team and Google Business Profile optimization guide lay out in more general terms.

Tracking pixels on a healthcare website: a real and still-evolving compliance risk

Most websites run a Meta Pixel and Google Ads conversion tracking without a second thought. On a healthcare website, that same code can constitute an impermissible disclosure of PHI. HHS OCR issued a bulletin on December 1, 2022 warning that tracking technologies — pixels, cookies, session-replay scripts — placed on pages where a user's health condition could reasonably be inferred (a symptom-specific landing page, a patient portal login, a specific provider's appointment booking flow) may transmit identifiable health information to the ad-tech vendor receiving that data, which OCR treated as a HIPAA violation when done without a proper authorization or business associate agreement. A 2024 federal court ruling, American Hospital Association v. Becerra, narrowed that bulletin — striking the portion that treated tracking on fully public, unauthenticated pages the same way as tracking on logged-in patient portals. The practical read for a provider today: tracking on a public services page describing what conditions a clinic treats is lower-risk than tracking anywhere a visitor has to log in or submit identifying symptom information, and the safest path is auditing exactly what each pixel can see on each page rather than assuming all tracking is equally fine, or equally risky.

This isn't an abstract regulatory footnote, either. The FTC has brought real enforcement against health-adjacent companies for exactly this pattern outside the hospital/clinic setting HIPAA covers: GoodRx paid a $1.5 million penalty in 2023 for sharing consumer health data with Facebook and Google without properly disclosing it, and the FTC separately barred Easy Healthcare Corporation, maker of the Premom fertility-tracking app, from sharing user health data for advertising after it had been sending that data to third parties without adequate notice. The FTC then finalized amendments to its Health Breach Notification Rule on April 26, 2024 (effective July 29, 2024) specifically to close the gap HIPAA leaves open — the rule now explicitly covers health apps, fitness trackers and similar consumer tools that fall outside HIPAA's scope, and it counts an unauthorized disclosure (not just a security breach) as a reportable event, with a 60-day notification clock once 500 or more people are affected.

Paid advertising: real, usable channels once the compliance basics are in place

None of the above is a reason to avoid paid ads — it's a reason to get the tracking setup right before scaling spend. Google Ads and Meta Ads both remain genuinely usable channels for most healthcare categories (general practice, dental, physical therapy, wellness), and the same PPC and Meta Ads fundamentals that apply everywhere else — tight geographic targeting, conversion tracking that's actually configured correctly, ad copy that matches what the landing page delivers — still apply here. The difference is simply that "configured correctly" has a compliance dimension for healthcare that it doesn't have for most other categories: verifying what each ad pixel and conversion tag can actually observe, keeping any PHI-adjacent form data out of ad-platform reporting, and routing genuinely sensitive intake flows through a properly covered, BAA-backed system rather than a generic form plugin.

Content marketing that builds authority without making claims it can't back

Educational content is one of the lowest-risk, highest-value channels available to a healthcare provider — a genuinely useful explainer on what a procedure involves, what insurance typically covers, or how to prepare for a visit answers real search intent without touching PHI at all, since it describes conditions and procedures in general terms rather than any specific patient. The same discipline that makes content work for any other industry applies here: answer the actual question a prospective patient is searching, link it to a real next step, and never dress up a marketing claim as a clinical one. Our content marketing team treats that distinction as a hard line for healthcare clients specifically, and it's also why a practice's website itself needs intake and contact forms built to keep symptom-specific detail off of channels — email marketing platforms, analytics dashboards, ad reporting — that were never built to handle PHI in the first place.

Mistakes that create real legal exposure, not just missed opportunity

  • Replying to a negative review with any identifying detail. The single most common and most litigated mistake, and the one with a real, named enforcement case behind it.
  • Running ad pixels on patient portals or symptom-specific pages without auditing what they transmit. HHS OCR's 2022 bulletin and the FTC's GoodRx and Premom actions both trace back to this exact pattern.
  • Treating intake and contact forms as generic lead-gen tools. A symptom-description field feeding into a standard CRM or ad platform is a PHI-handling decision, whether or not it was made consciously.
  • No written, pre-approved review-response policy. Leaving it to whoever's logged into the Google Business Profile that day is how a well-meaning staff response becomes a federal case.
  • Treating educational content as a place to make clinical promises. The marketing and legal risk both rise the moment a blog post or ad implies a specific outcome rather than describing a general service.

Where to actually start

In order: write and train staff on a pre-approved, PHI-free review-response policy first, since it's free, fast to fix, and the single most common real violation; audit exactly what every tracking pixel and analytics script on the website can see, especially on any patient portal, symptom-specific or appointment-booking page, second; complete and properly categorize the Google Business Profile third, since it's the free local-visibility lever with the highest leverage once reviews are being handled safely; layer in PPC and Meta Ads fourth, once conversion tracking has actually been checked rather than assumed safe; and build out educational content fifth, as the lowest-risk, longest-compounding channel once the compliance foundation underneath it is solid. Most of the real damage in this industry doesn't come from under-marketing — it comes from marketing fast without checking what the compliance layer underneath it is actually doing.

Frequently asked questions

Can a medical practice respond to a negative online review?

Yes, but not with any protected health information, and legal commentary on HHS OCR's enforcement record is clear that this includes simply confirming the reviewer was a patient at all — HIPAA does not permit that confirmation even if the reviewer has already said so themselves. A safe reply thanks the reviewer for feedback, says nothing confirming a treatment relationship, and invites them to call the office directly. HHS OCR's June 2023 settlement with Manasa Health Center, a New Jersey psychiatric practice that paid $30,000 after disclosing patient information in responses to four patients' negative reviews, is the clearest real example of what happens when that line gets crossed.

Is it safe to run the Meta Pixel or Google Ads tracking on a healthcare website?

It depends heavily on what pages the tracking sits on and what data it can see. HHS OCR's December 2022 bulletin warned that tracking technologies on patient portals or any page where a user's health condition could reasonably be inferred may count as an impermissible disclosure of protected health information to the ad-tech vendor. A 2024 federal court ruling (American Hospital Association v. Becerra) narrowed that bulletin, striking the part that treated tracking on unauthenticated public pages the same way — but the core risk on logged-in patient portals and appointment-booking flows remains very real, and the FTC has separately fined companies like GoodRx for sharing health data with Facebook and Google without proper disclosure.

Does HIPAA apply to a clinic's own marketing website?

HIPAA applies to protected health information handled by covered entities and their vendors — not to marketing copy itself. A general services page describing what a clinic treats isn't PHI. The risk shows up specifically where the site collects or transmits identifiable patient data: contact forms asking about symptoms, patient portal logins, appointment schedulers tied to a specific provider, and any analytics or ad pixel placed on those pages without a proper business associate agreement or de-identification.
