What Actually Moves Local Search Rankings? Whitespark's 2026 Ranking Factors

Google has never published the exact formula behind its local pack and Maps rankings, which is exactly why Whitespark's Local Search Ranking Factors survey has become one of the most-cited reference points in local SEO: it doesn't claim to reverse-engineer the algorithm, it aggregates what dozens of working local SEO practitioners genuinely believe is moving rankings, based on what they see across real client accounts. The edition covering 2026, published November 6, 2025 by Whitespark's Darren Shaw, was the first update to the survey in just over two years — and it added an entirely new category that didn't exist in the previous edition.

Whitespark's Local Search Ranking Factors survey asked 47 local search experts to spend roughly two hours each scoring 187 individual ranking factors — and for 2026, it added a brand-new category, AI Search visibility, that didn't exist in the prior edition. Published November 6, 2025 and authored by Whitespark's Darren Shaw, it's the first update to this specific survey in just over two years, which on its own is a signal worth noting: the gap reflects how much local search itself has shifted in that window, enough that the previous factor framework reportedly needed real revision, not just a refresh of the same categories with updated numbers.

What the survey actually measures, and what it doesn't

It's worth being precise about what this report is, because it gets cited constantly and sometimes gets cited as more than it claims to be. It is not Google disclosing its ranking algorithm — Google has never done that for local search, and there's no indication it's starting now. It is not a controlled correlation study running statistical analysis against millions of real ranking positions, the way some broader SEO industry studies attempt. It is a structured expert-opinion survey: a panel of working local SEO practitioners, people who manage real local search campaigns for a living, independently scoring how much weight they believe Google assigns to each of 187 specific ranking factors, organized into broader factor groups.

That distinction matters for how much confidence to place in any specific number the report produces. Expert consensus among experienced practitioners is genuinely useful — it's closer to ground truth than guessing, and it's the best aggregated signal generally available precisely because Google doesn't publish the real answer. But it's still opinion, triangulated from what different practitioners have separately observed working (and failing) across their own client portfolios, not measured directly from Google's systems. Keep that framing in mind through everything below.

The four categories, including the one that's new this year

The 2026 edition organizes its 187 factors into four weighted groups, asking participants to estimate how much weight Google gives each group for two different result types — the local pack/Maps results, and standard local organic results — plus two additional dimensions:

Local pack/Maps ranking impact — what moves a business into the map-and-three-listings block that appears for most "near me" and city-plus-service searches, generally the highest-visibility, highest-click real estate for a local service business.

Local organic ranking impact — what moves a business's actual website higher in the standard blue-link results for locally-intended queries, a related but distinct ranking system from the map pack.

Conversion impact — a category specifically about what makes a searcher who sees your listing actually act (call, visit, click through, request a quote), as opposed to factors that only affect whether you're seen at all. This distinction matters in practice: a business can rank well and still underperform competitors on actual leads if its profile doesn't convert attention into contact.

AI Search visibility (new for 2026) — a category that didn't exist as its own weighted group in the prior edition, covering how well a business surfaces inside AI-generated answers rather than traditional pack or organic results. Its addition as a standalone category, rather than as a footnote inside local organic, is itself a notable finding: practitioners now consider AI-generated local answers a materially different visibility surface, with at least partly different drivers, not simply an extension of classic local pack optimization.

What practitioners reportedly weighted highest — with an honest caveat

Multiple industry outlets that covered the report's findings — including Loganix and BIZiQ's write-ups of the survey — converged on a consistent picture: Google Business Profile signals (completeness, categories, services, attributes, posts, Q&A, photo volume, and related profile-level factors) remain the single largest weighted group for local pack results, with review signals and on-page signals following behind it. Separately, Moz's own long-running benchmark, also cited alongside this report in third-party coverage, has tracked citation-related ranking weight declining across multiple survey cycles — reportedly down from roughly 13% in 2020 to a meaningfully smaller share by the 2026 edition's comparison point. We're citing these specific figures as reported by outlets covering the study rather than lifting them directly from a single page of Whitespark's own site, since the report's full breakdown lives behind its detailed commentary pages — but the direction these multiple independent write-ups agree on (GBP signals dominant, citations continuing a multi-year decline, proximity remaining one of the strongest individual single factors) is consistent enough across sources to treat as a reliable summary of what the study found, even where an exact percentage point might vary slightly by which specific write-up you're reading.

Proximity — literally how close the searcher is to your business location at the moment they search — is reported as one of the single strongest individual local pack factors, and it's also the one lever a business has essentially zero direct control over. That's worth internalizing before chasing it: the actionable part of local SEO isn't proximity, it's everything else on this list that's actually within your control.

What this means for a local business's actual priorities

Google Business Profile completeness isn't a one-time setup task. If GBP signals carry the heaviest weight of any single factor group, then an incomplete profile, stale categories, missing services, or Q&A nobody's monitoring is a bigger drag on visibility than most on-page tweaks could offset. This is ongoing maintenance, not a box to check once at launch.

Reviews need volume, recency, and response — not just a high average rating. A profile with forty reviews from eighteen months ago reads differently to both Google and a searcher than one with a steady, recent trickle of new reviews that the business visibly responds to. The practitioners behind this kind of survey consistently flag recency and response behavior as part of what they're scoring, not just the star average itself.

On-page signals still matter, but can't carry a weak profile. If on-page SEO sits behind GBP and reviews in relative weight, the practical implication isn't "don't bother with on-page" — it's that on-page work is unlikely to compensate for a thin or unverified Business Profile. Fix the profile first; then on-page work has something solid to build visibility on top of.

AI Search visibility is a new, separate skill worth starting on now, even while it's still maturing. Because this category didn't exist in the prior survey edition, there's less established best-practice around it than around classic local pack optimization — but its appearance as its own category is a signal that practitioners are already treating it as a distinct, trackable part of local visibility, not a future concern to defer.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Whitespark Local Search Ranking Factors report?

It's a survey-based study, published by Whitespark and authored by Darren Shaw, in which a panel of local SEO practitioners score how much weight they believe Google gives to different ranking factor groups for local pack, Maps, and local organic results. The edition covering 2026 was published November 6, 2025, surveying 47 experts across 187 individual factors, and was the first update to the survey in just over two years.

What changed in this edition specifically?

The 2026 edition added AI Search visibility as its own separate weighted category for the first time, alongside the existing local pack/Maps impact, local organic impact, and conversion impact categories — reflecting that practitioners now see AI-generated search answers as a distinct factor in local visibility, not just an extension of traditional map pack or organic rankings.

Does this report reflect Google's actual ranking algorithm?

No, and this is an important distinction. The report is explicitly an aggregated expert-opinion survey, not a disclosure from Google or a controlled correlation study against real ranking data. Google does not publish the exact weighting of its local ranking signals, so this kind of practitioner consensus is the closest approximation generally available, not confirmed ground truth.
