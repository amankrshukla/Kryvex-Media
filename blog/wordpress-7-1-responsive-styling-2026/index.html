WordPress 7.1 Adds Built-In Responsive Styling. Here Is What Actually Changes.

WordPress 7.1, codenamed Mary Lou, puts responsive design controls directly in the Site Editor: a device-preview toggle that lets you adjust how a block looks at different breakpoints without writing a line of custom CSS. That's a real shift for a platform where per-breakpoint tweaks used to mean either a developer or a plugin. Here is what the feature covers, what it doesn't, and what to actually check on your own site.

K
Kryvex Media Team
Nov 13, 2026 · 9 min read

If your website runs on WordPress, which is still the platform behind a large share of small business sites, a feature in the newest release quietly removes one of the most common reasons a design tweak used to require calling a developer. WordPress 7.1, officially codenamed Mary Lou per the project's official announcement on wordpress.org, adds responsive styling controls built directly into the block editor's Site Editor. It's a narrower change than it might sound, and understanding exactly what it does and doesn't cover matters more than the headline. WordPress still powers a very large share of the web's small business sites, which means a core platform change like this one ripples out to a lot of day to day website work far faster than a similar change on a smaller platform would.

What the feature actually does

Per the official wordpress.org announcement and the development roadmap published on make.wordpress.org, WordPress 7.1 adds a device-preview icon directly in the Site Editor toolbar. Clicking it and toggling "Responsive styles" lets you select a block and adjust how it looks at a given breakpoint, spacing, sizing, and layout among the controls, without writing custom CSS or media queries by hand. Previously, achieving the same result, making a block behave differently on mobile than on desktop, required either a developer writing custom CSS, a page builder plugin layered on top of WordPress core, or accepting whatever the theme's built-in responsive behavior happened to be.

A second change aimed at theme developers, not site owners directly

The same release adds pseudo-state styling, meaning hover, focus, focus-visible, and active states, configurable through a theme's theme.json file rather than requiring hand-coded CSS for each state. This one is mostly relevant if you or your developer builds or customizes a WordPress block theme rather than using one as-is. One third-party report claims this pseudo-state control is currently limited to button blocks specifically in this release, a detail not explicitly confirmed in the official wordpress.org announcement itself, so treat that scope limitation as likely but not fully verified until you check it directly against your own theme's behavior.

The release also includes customizable viewport breakpoints and improved media handling, per the official roadmap post. The specific scope of "improved media handling" wasn't detailed in the sources reviewed for this piece beyond that general description, so don't assume it covers a specific workflow you have in mind until you've tested it directly after updating.

Why this matters even if you never touch the editor yourself

Most small business owners don't personally sit in the WordPress editor adjusting blocks. The reason this release still matters to you is cost and turnaround time on your side of the relationship with whoever maintains your site. A request like "make this section smaller on mobile" that used to require a developer writing and testing custom CSS, billing for the time, can now often be handled directly inside the editor in minutes, by someone with far less specialized CSS knowledge than before. That changes what a reasonable turnaround time and cost looks like for small, cosmetic responsive adjustments going forward, whether you handle it yourself or ask your agency to.

Why this connects to more than just design preferences

Responsive behavior isn't purely a cosmetic concern. Google has used mobile-friendliness and page experience signals as part of how it evaluates search results for years, and a majority of search traffic for most small businesses now arrives on a phone rather than a desktop. A block that looks fine on a laptop screen but overflows, overlaps, or forces horizontal scrolling on mobile creates exactly the kind of experience that hurts both conversion rates and, over time, how a page performs in search. Making responsive adjustments easier to actually ship, rather than something that gets deprioritized because it requires a developer and a billing conversation, removes a real reason those fixes used to sit in a backlog instead of getting done, since a site owner who can make the change directly has no reason to wait on a developer's availability for something this small. That's a genuine, if indirect, SEO and conversion benefit sitting behind what reads on the surface as a developer convenience feature.

What this doesn't change

This feature operates within WordPress's block editor and Site Editor system, meaning it applies to block themes, not older classic themes built around traditional PHP templates and the Customizer. A site running an older classic theme won't gain these specific controls just by updating WordPress core; the theme itself needs to be a block theme that uses the Site Editor. It also doesn't replace the value of genuinely custom development for anything beyond adjusting existing blocks: new functionality, performance optimization, accessibility fixes, and structural redesigns still benefit from an experienced developer who understands the platform, this feature just narrows the specific gap of basic per-breakpoint styling.

What to actually check on your own site

Confirm whether your current theme is a block theme. If it's an older classic theme, this specific feature won't be available to you without a theme change, even after updating WordPress core itself.

Test the responsive toggle on a staging copy before relying on it for client-facing edits. A new editor feature in its first release is exactly the kind of thing worth verifying on a non-live copy of your site first, not your actual homepage.

Ask your developer or agency what this changes about your support arrangement. If you're paying for ongoing small CSS tweaks, this is a reasonable moment to ask whether that scope and cost should shift now that some of that work can happen without custom code.

What this means for the WordPress plugin ecosystem

A real, if less immediately visible, effect of this release sits with the page builder and responsive design plugin ecosystem that grew up specifically to fill the gap WordPress core didn't cover. Plugins built around visual responsive controls for existing WordPress sites built their value proposition partly on exactly this gap: making per-breakpoint adjustments possible without hand-written CSS. WordPress core closing part of that gap doesn't make those plugins obsolete outright, many offer broader layout systems, templating, and design controls well beyond basic responsive styling, but it does mean a business evaluating whether it still needs a dedicated page builder plugin, purely for responsive adjustments on an otherwise standard block theme site, now has a real core-feature alternative to weigh against the plugin's cost, maintenance overhead, and potential performance impact.

Frequently asked questions

What does WordPress 7.1's responsive styling feature actually do?

It adds a device-preview toggle directly in the Site Editor that lets you turn on responsive styles and adjust how a block looks at different breakpoints, such as spacing, size, or layout, without writing custom CSS. Previously, that kind of per-breakpoint adjustment required a developer to hand code media queries.

Does this mean small business owners no longer need a developer for their WordPress site?

No, it narrows one specific gap, not the whole relationship. It removes the need for custom CSS for basic per-breakpoint adjustments to existing blocks, but a site built on an outdated theme, with real performance problems, or needing new functionality, still benefits from a developer or agency who understands the platform.

Do I need to do anything to get WordPress 7.1's new responsive styling controls?

You need to update to WordPress 7.1, codenamed Mary Lou, once it's available for your site, and you need a block theme that uses the Site Editor rather than an older classic theme. Classic themes built around traditional PHP templates and the older Customizer won't show the same device-preview responsive controls.
