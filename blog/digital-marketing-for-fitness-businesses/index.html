Digital Marketing for Fitness Businesses: What the FTC Actually Restricts

Gyms, studios and independent trainers sell on exactly the content that draws the most regulatory attention: transformation photos, membership offers, and claims about what a program or product will do to a client's body. Three real, current rule sets govern that content — how membership cancellation has to work, what a trainer's before-and-after post has to disclose, and what evidence a weight-loss or supplement claim actually needs behind it — and a 2025 federal lawsuit against one of the country's largest gym chains shows the FTC is actively enforcing the first of those right now. None of this means a fitness business should market less aggressively. It means knowing exactly where the real lines sit.

A gym, studio or independent trainer runs marketing on three kinds of content that other local businesses rarely touch in the same way: a recurring membership offer, a transformation or testimonial post, and a claim about what a program, class or supplement does to a client's body. Each of those has a real, current regulatory layer sitting underneath it. Membership and auto-renewal marketing runs into the FTC's negative-option enforcement and a growing list of state-level gym-cancellation laws — one of which produced a federal lawsuit against a major chain in August 2025. Transformation and trainer-endorsement content runs into the FTC's 2023-revised Endorsement Guides. And any claim tying a product, program or supplement to weight loss or a health outcome runs into the FTC's current Health Products Compliance Guidance, which demands real scientific substantiation, not a client's word for it. The underlying growth mechanics — a strong Google Business Profile, paid search for high-intent local searches, content that answers real questions, email to an opted-in list — work the same way they do for any other local business. What's different, and covered below with the real current rules, is the compliance layer that sits on top of them.

Membership marketing and the cancellation problem regulators are now enforcing

Recurring membership revenue is the backbone of most gym and studio business models, and it is also the single area of fitness marketing currently drawing the most direct federal attention. On August 20, 2025, the FTC filed a federal complaint against Fitness International LLC and its subsidiary Fitness & Sports Clubs LLC — the operators of LA Fitness, along with Esporta Fitness, City Sports Club and Club Studio, more than 600 locations and over 3.7 million members nationwide. The complaint alleges the company made cancellation deliberately difficult — limiting it in practice to in-person requests during restricted hours or to a mailed form that had to be printed from a site some customers couldn't log into — while continuing to charge recurring fees the FTC says added up to hundreds of millions of dollars collected from members who were trying to cancel. The agency's claims rest on Section 5 of the FTC Act and the Restore Online Shoppers' Confidence Act (ROSCA), and it is seeking both an order barring the practices and refunds for affected consumers. Whatever the final outcome of that specific case, the complaint itself is a real, current statement of what the FTC considers an unfair negative-option practice in the fitness industry specifically, not a hypothetical risk.

This sits alongside a separate, real enforcement note from North Carolina, where the state alleged a gym sold prepaid memberships without honoring the three-day right to cancel state law requires — a reminder that cancellation rules in this industry come from both federal and state sources, and a prepaid or paid-in-full membership isn't exempt just because no recurring billing is involved.

The federal click-to-cancel rule is vacated — here's what still actually applies

Marketing teams that tracked the FTC's 2024 "click-to-cancel" rule — which would have required that canceling a subscription or membership be as easy as signing up for one — should know its status changed in 2025. The Eighth Circuit vacated the rule in its entirety in Custom Communications, Inc. v. FTC (No. 24-3137, decided July 8, 2025), ruling the FTC skipped a required preliminary regulatory-impact analysis once the rule's own projected cost crossed $100 million a year. The rule is not in effect, and the FTC has not announced a replacement as of this writing.

That does not mean auto-renewing memberships are unregulated. Three real backstops remain live right now: Section 5 of the FTC Act still lets the agency pursue deceptive or unfair negative-option practices case by case — exactly the theory behind the LA Fitness complaint above, which was filed under the FTC Act rather than the vacated rule; ROSCA still applies to online negative-option offers and requires clear disclosure of the offer's terms, consent before charging, and a simple cancellation mechanism; and the FTC's original 1973 negative-option rule remains in force for mail and telephone subscription plans. On top of all three, state law fills the gap the vacated federal rule left — California already has its own click-to-cancel statute for consumer transactions, and New York's health-club-specific law (effective February 1, 2025, under General Business Law §624(2)) independently requires gyms to let members cancel online, honor a cooling-off window shortly after signing without penalty, and process cancellations and refunds within a set number of business days. A fitness business operating in, or signing members from, any state with its own cancellation statute needs its actual cancellation flow — not just its terms-of-service language — to match that state's rule, regardless of what happens to the federal rule next.

Run your actual cancellation process the way a new member would — not the way the terms of service describe it. The FTC's complaint against LA Fitness is specifically about the gap between a policy on paper and what members experience trying to use it. If cancellation requires a specific person, a specific window, or a form a member can't easily access online, that gap is the real exposure, independent of which federal rule is or isn't currently in force.

Transformation posts, trainer testimonials and the FTC's Endorsement Guides

Before-and-after content is close to universal in fitness marketing, and it is governed directly by the FTC's Guides Concerning the Use of Endorsements and Testimonials in Advertising (16 CFR Part 255), which the agency revised for the first time since 2009 on June 29, 2023 (published in the Federal Register July 26, 2023, at 88 FR 48102). Two separate obligations come out of that revision. First, §255.5's material-connection rule: if a client, trainer or influencer posting a transformation or review has any connection to the business that a typical viewer wouldn't assume — payment, free training, a comped or discounted membership, anything of value, even without an explicit requirement to post in return — that connection has to be disclosed clearly and conspicuously, not buried behind a "more" link or a platform's own default disclosure tool, which the FTC's own guidance and staff materials note may not be prominent enough on its own.

Second, and specific to this industry: the revised Guides state that an advertiser must disclose when a depicted endorsement doesn't reflect the results a typical person can expect, and must not imply that extraordinary results are generally achievable. The FTC's own position, stated directly in the revision, is that a generic "results not typical" disclaimer is likely insufficient on its own — the fix is actually disclosing what someone following the same program would realistically expect to achieve, not just flagging that the photo shown is unusual. For a gym or trainer whose entire content strategy runs on dramatic transformation photos, that is a direct, specific compliance obligation, not a general best practice. Liability under the Guides isn't limited to the business, either — the FTC's materials note that the endorser and any marketing intermediary involved can also be held responsible for a deceptive endorsement, alongside the advertiser.

Weight-loss and health claims: the "competent and reliable scientific evidence" bar

Any claim tying a membership, program or retail supplement to weight loss, fat reduction or a health outcome runs into a separate FTC standard entirely. In December 2022, the FTC replaced its 1998 dietary-supplement advertising guide with its current Health Products Compliance Guidance, and deliberately broadened its scope beyond supplements to any health-related claim — covering functional foods, over-the-counter products, fitness devices and health-tracking apps along with supplements themselves. The core requirement is that a health claim be backed by "competent and reliable scientific evidence" — studies that experts in the relevant field would objectively judge adequate for the specific claim being made, evaluated against the whole body of relevant research, not a single favorable study. For claims specifically about weight loss, fat-blocking or metabolism effects, the guidance states that substantiation will generally need to include randomized, controlled human clinical testing — client testimonials, before-and-after photos or a trainer's personal results are not a substitute for that evidence, regardless of how genuine they are. The FDA separately regulates product labeling and safety; the FTC's jurisdiction here is specifically the advertising claim itself, which means the marketing team — not just a product's manufacturer — carries real exposure for an unsubstantiated claim in an ad, a landing page or a social post.

Where the marketing mechanics are the same as any other local business

Once membership-cancellation flows, endorsement disclosures and health-claim substantiation are built into the workflow, a fitness business grows the same way any other local service business does. A complete, accurately categorized Google Business Profile — correct class schedules, real amenities, genuine member reviews — remains a free, high-leverage starting point, the same foundation our local SEO team builds for every local client, fitness or otherwise. Paid search continues to perform well for high-intent local queries ("gym near me," a specific class type, a specific trainer specialty), as long as the landing page's membership terms and cancellation process match what the ad actually promises. Social media and Meta ads remain the highest-leverage channels for a visual business like this one — provided every paid or incentivized post carries the material-connection disclosure the Endorsement Guides require. Content marketing — real training guides, class-format explainers, honest discussion of what a program can and can't do — is one of the lowest-risk channels precisely because it doesn't depend on a single dramatic result to work. And email marketing to an opted-in member list remains largely untouched by the rules above, since it isn't a public endorsement and isn't a new-member cancellation point.

Mistakes that create real compliance exposure, not just missed opportunity

  • Limiting membership cancellation to in-person visits or a mailed form while continuing to bill recurring fees — this is the exact fact pattern in the FTC's August 2025 complaint against the LA Fitness operators.
  • Running a comped-membership or free-training influencer arrangement without a disclosed material connection — §255.5 requires disclosure whenever a typical viewer wouldn't otherwise assume the connection exists, even without an explicit posting requirement.
  • Posting a dramatic transformation with only a "results not typical" disclaimer — the FTC's 2023 revision treats that generic disclaimer as likely insufficient; the fix is disclosing what a typical client can actually expect.
  • Making a weight-loss, fat-loss or metabolism claim backed only by testimonials — the Health Products Compliance Guidance calls for randomized, controlled human clinical evidence for claims in this specific category.
  • Assuming the vacated federal click-to-cancel rule means auto-renewal is unregulated — the FTC Act, ROSCA, the 1973 negative-option rule and state-specific laws like New York's all remain fully in force regardless of that one rule's status.

Where to actually start

In order: test your own membership cancellation flow the way a real member would, end to end, and fix any step that requires a specific person, a specific window, or a form that isn't actually accessible online; audit every paid or comped influencer and trainer post currently live for a clear material-connection disclosure under §255.5; review transformation content for a real, typical-results disclosure rather than a generic disclaimer; confirm every weight-loss or health-outcome claim in current ads and landing pages traces to real clinical evidence, not a testimonial; then build out the Google Business Profile, paid search, social and email layers that work the same way they do for any other local business, now that the content feeding them is clean. The fitness businesses growing fastest right now aren't the ones pulling back from transformation marketing or membership offers — they're the ones that fixed the cancellation flow and the disclosure gap before a complaint, a chargeback wave or a state inquiry forced the issue.

Frequently asked questions

Can a gym still auto-renew memberships after the FTC's click-to-cancel rule was vacated?

The federal click-to-cancel rule itself was vacated by the Eighth Circuit in Custom Communications, Inc. v. FTC (July 8, 2025) over a procedural defect in how the FTC adopted it, so that specific rule is not in effect. That does not mean auto-renewal is unregulated: Section 5 of the FTC Act still lets the agency treat deceptive or unfair negative-option practices as illegal on a case-by-case basis, the Restore Online Shoppers' Confidence Act (ROSCA) still applies to online auto-renewing offers, the FTC's 1973 negative-option rule still governs mail and telephone subscription plans, and several states (California among them, with New York's own health-club-specific law already in effect) have their own cancellation statutes that don't depend on the federal rule at all.

What does the FTC's 2023 update to the Endorsement Guides actually require for a trainer's before-and-after post?

Two separate disclosures. First, under 16 CFR 255.5, any material connection between the business and the person posting — payment, free training, a discounted membership, anything of value — has to be disclosed clearly if a typical viewer wouldn't otherwise assume it exists. Second, under the substance of the 2023 revision, a transformation post that doesn't reflect what most clients can realistically expect has to disclose the results a typical person would actually get; the FTC's own guidance says a generic "results not typical" disclaimer is unlikely to satisfy this on its own.

Can a gym or trainer claim a supplement or program causes weight loss in an ad?

Only with real substantiation behind the specific claim. The FTC's Health Products Compliance Guidance (issued December 2022, replacing the agency's 1998 dietary-supplement guide) applies to any health-related claim, not just supplements, and requires "competent and reliable scientific evidence" — evidence a relevant expert would judge adequate given the type of claim being made. For claims about a product's effect on weight, fat loss or metabolism specifically, the guidance states that substantiation will generally need to include randomized, controlled human clinical testing, not testimonials or anecdotal results.
