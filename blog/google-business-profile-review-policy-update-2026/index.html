Google's April 2026 Review Policy Update: What Changed on Google Business Profile

Around mid-April 2026, Google added two new clauses to the prohibited-content section of its Google Business Profile and Maps review policy, both aimed at a specific practice: businesses directing staff to solicit reviews on command. One bans setting a review quota for staff to hit. The other bans directing staff to solicit reviews with specific requested content, including naming a staff member. Coverage of the update has since drifted into some overstated claims — that any staff name in a review is now banned outright, for instance — that don't match Google's own policy language. Here's what Google's policy actually says, what's genuinely disputed, and what a small business should change.

In mid-April 2026, Google added two new clauses to the Business Profile and Maps prohibited-content policy, both targeting the same underlying practice: a business directing its own staff to solicit reviews in a specific, scripted way. The first bans merchants from directing staff to solicit a specific number or quota of reviews. The second bans directing staff to solicit reviews with specific requested content — which the policy update names explicitly as including content that identifies a particular staff member. Trade coverage dating the change to around April 17, 2026 (reported by ppc.land, among others) frames it correctly as a quota-and-scripting ban, not a ban on reviews ever mentioning an employee's name. That distinction has gotten blurred in some of the secondary coverage since, and it's worth untangling directly because getting it wrong leads to the wrong fix.

What the two new clauses actually say

Google's Business Profile help documentation on prohibited and restricted content is the controlling source here, and the two additions can be stated plainly:

  • No staff review quotas. A business cannot direct or require staff to solicit a specific number of reviews — a policy that targets the common "get X reviews this week" internal target some businesses set for front-line staff, treating that quota itself as a form of review manipulation regardless of whether the resulting reviews are genuine.
  • No staff-scripted solicitation with specific content, including names. A business cannot direct staff to solicit reviews with specific requested content — and the update specifically calls out content that identifies a staff member as an example of what that covers. In practice, this targets a business coaching staff to say something like "ask the customer to mention you by name in their review," not the review itself.

Both are enforcement-side clarifications of a position Google has held for years: reviews should reflect a genuine customer's own, un-coached experience, and businesses manufacturing the inputs to that process — quotas, scripts, specific requested wording — undermine the signal reviews are supposed to carry. Google has prohibited broader "review gating" (selectively asking only happy customers to leave public reviews while routing unhappy ones elsewhere) since 2018; this update extends the same underlying principle to quota-setting and content-scripting specifically.

What this update does not do, despite some overstated secondary coverage: it does not ban a customer from voluntarily writing a staff member's name in their own review. A customer who genuinely wants to credit "Priya at the front desk" for great service, entirely of their own accord, has not triggered anything in this policy. The prohibited act is the business directing staff to solicit that specific content — not the content itself appearing because a real customer chose to include it.

Why the "staff names are banned" version of this story spread

Several marketing and compliance blogs covering the April update compressed "businesses can't script staff to request reviews naming an employee" into the much broader, inaccurate claim that reviews mentioning employees are now against policy outright, or that Google is actively removing any existing review that names staff. That framing doesn't match the policy text, and at least two sources covering the update directly pushed back on it — one calling the "staff mention ban" framing misleading, another pointing out that rewarding a technician for delivering good service, where a customer organically credits that technician by name afterward, is a completely different thing from directing staff to solicit that exact outcome. The enforcement and retroactivity question — whether Google will act on existing, already-published reviews that happen to name staff — also remains genuinely unclear in the public reporting; nothing here claims certainty on that point because the certainty isn't there to claim.

How this connects to Google's broader review-manipulation enforcement

This update sits inside a wider pattern Google has been building out over 2026: review-gating enforcement that's reportedly become more automated and more consistent at catching patterns businesses previously got away with. The practical throughline for any business optimizing a Google Business Profile is the same one Google has stated for years, just enforced more precisely now — ask for reviews, don't engineer them. Asking a satisfied customer to leave a review is fine. Incentivizing reviews with discounts or gifts is not, and remains explicitly prohibited regardless of this update. Setting an internal quota for staff, or scripting exactly what staff should ask a customer to write, is the new, explicitly named violation this update adds to that existing list.

What a small business should actually change

Three concrete adjustments, grounded in what the policy actually says rather than the overstated version:

  • Drop any internal review quota tied to staff performance. If a manager currently tracks "reviews generated" as a KPI per staff member with a numeric target, that's precisely the practice the first clause targets — not the act of asking customers for reviews at all, but the quota structure around it.
  • Stop scripting exact review content for staff to request. A general "if you have a moment, a Google review helps us a lot" ask is fine. A script instructing staff to specifically ask customers to mention that staff member by name, or to include specific phrases, is the second clause's target.
  • Keep doing the things Google has never prohibited: genuinely asking happy customers for a review without dictating content, responding to reviews publicly and professionally, and letting satisfied customers credit whoever they want to credit, in their own words, without coaching.

Where the real uncertainty still sits

Honest caveat: public reporting on this update, even from sources trying to get it right, disagrees on enforcement specifics — whether automated detection is already flagging profiles at scale, whether there's a numeric threshold (like "90% of reviews mention an employee name") that triggers scrutiny, and how Google treats reviews published before the policy change. None of those specifics are confirmed by Google's own documentation as of this writing, and a business shouldn't restructure its entire review strategy around an unconfirmed enforcement detail. The two confirmed rule changes — no quotas, no scripted content requests — are the actual, verifiable standard to build around.

The bigger local SEO picture

Reviews remain one of the more influential signals in local search visibility, which is exactly why Google keeps tightening the rules around how they're collected rather than discouraging review collection altogether. For a small business, the sustainable approach hasn't actually changed with this update — it's just gotten a more explicit rulebook. Consistently good service that customers want to talk about, paired with a simple, undictated ask for a review, was always the strategy that holds up under scrutiny; this update mainly removes the shortcuts (quotas, scripts) that were never a durable substitute for that anyway. Frequently asked questions

What did Google actually change in its April 2026 review policy update?

Google added two clauses to its Business Profile prohibited and restricted content policy around mid-April 2026. The first prohibits merchants from directing staff to solicit a specific number or quota of reviews. The second prohibits merchants from directing staff to solicit reviews containing specific content, including content that identifies a particular staff member by name. Both are enforcement clarifications to Google's existing ban on review manipulation, not a reversal of Google's long-standing position that genuine customer reviews are welcome.

Does this mean a customer can never mention an employee's name in a review anymore?

No, and this is the most commonly misreported part of the update. The new rule targets a business directing or coaching staff to solicit reviews with specific content, such as a name. A customer who voluntarily and organically writes an employee's name into their own review because that's genuinely who helped them has not violated anything, and multiple outlets covering the update, including vendor compliance blogs, distinguish this clearly from the prohibited behavior.

What should a small business actually stop doing because of this update?

Stop any internal practice that sets a review quota for staff (for example, "get 5 reviews a week" as a target tied to performance) and stop scripting or coaching staff on exactly what to ask customers to write, especially instructions to mention a specific staff member by name. Continuing to deliver genuinely good service that customers want to write about organically, and asking for a review without dictating its content, both remain fully within Google's policy.
