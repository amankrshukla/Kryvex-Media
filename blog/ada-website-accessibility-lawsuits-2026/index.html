Website Accessibility Lawsuits Rose 27% in 2025: What Small Business Owners Should Know

Seyfarth Shaw, a law firm that has tracked ADA Title III litigation for years, reported 3,117 federal website accessibility lawsuits filed in 2025 — a 27% increase over 2024, and 36% of all 8,667 federal ADA Title III lawsuits filed that year. This isn't a hypothetical risk aimed only at large corporations; it's a real, growing category of litigation that small business websites increasingly sit inside. Here's what the actual numbers show, what standard courts use to judge a website, and what genuinely reduces the risk versus what only looks like it does.

K
Kryvex Media Team
Oct 30, 2026 · 10 min read

Seyfarth Shaw's annual tracker — published in its March 2026 report, "Federal Court Website Accessibility Lawsuit Filings Bounce Back in 2025," and republished on JD Supra and Mondaq — counted 3,117 website accessibility lawsuits filed in U.S. federal court in 2025, a 27% increase over 2024. That figure made up 36% of all federal ADA Title III lawsuits filed that year (8,667 total, itself down about 2% from 2024) — meaning more than a third of all federal disability-access litigation in 2025 specifically concerned a website, not a physical location. For any small business whose site is its storefront, waiting room, or booking desk, that's a real and growing category of legal exposure, not an abstract compliance checkbox.

The numbers, and why different trackers report different totals

Seyfarth's 3,117 figure counts federal court filings only. Other organizations that track this space, such as UsableNet, publish higher totals because they also count filings in state courts, which some plaintiffs' attorneys prefer for procedural reasons in certain states. Neither number is wrong — they're measuring different things, and the gap between them is a methodology difference, not a disagreement about the underlying trend. Both trackers agree on the direction: website accessibility litigation grew significantly in 2025 after a dip in 2024, and 2026 filing activity reported by industry trackers through the year has continued at a comparable pace.

36% of all federal ADA Title III lawsuits in 2025 were specifically about a website. That's the single number worth internalizing from Seyfarth's report — website accessibility isn't a niche corner of disability-access litigation anymore, it's over a third of it.

What standard do courts actually use?

The ADA itself, written in 1990, doesn't name a specific technical standard for websites — it wasn't written with the modern web in mind. In practice, both courts and plaintiffs' attorneys have converged on WCAG (Web Content Accessibility Guidelines), most commonly version 2.1 at the AA conformance level, as the practical yardstick for what "accessible" means. WCAG 2.1 AA covers concrete, testable requirements: sufficient color contrast between text and background, full keyboard navigability (not just mouse/touch), descriptive alt text on meaningful images, a logical heading structure that screen readers can navigate, and accessible form labels, among others. None of these are abstract design preferences — they're specific, auditable items a developer can check a site against.

Who's actually getting sued

E-commerce sites are consistently reported as the most frequently sued category across the trackers covering this space, which makes intuitive sense: an online store that a visually impaired or motor-impaired customer can't actually use to browse or check out is a direct, demonstrable barrier to a core business function, and that's the clearest fact pattern for this type of claim. But plenty of 2025 and 2026 filings target businesses with no e-commerce function at all — a restaurant's reservation page, a clinic's appointment booking form, a local service business's contact form — anywhere a website visitor is expected to complete an action and can't because of how the site was built. Small businesses are not a protected category here; multiple trackers covering this space explicitly note smaller companies are frequent, not occasional, targets.

The overlay-widget trap

One pattern worth calling out directly because it affects a specific, common small-business decision: accessibility overlay widgets — the small floating icon that adds a menu of display adjustments to an existing site without touching its underlying code — are a popular quick fix, but available industry reporting on recent lawsuit activity describes a meaningful share of sued businesses as already having one installed at the time they were sued. The reason isn't mysterious: an overlay can add surface-level adjustments (font size, contrast toggles) without actually fixing the underlying code-level issues — missing alt text, broken keyboard navigation, improperly labeled form fields — that WCAG 2.1 AA and most plaintiffs' complaints actually test for. An overlay is not the same thing as a genuine accessibility audit and remediation, and treating it as equivalent is a real, documented risk, not a hypothetical one.

What actually reduces the risk

  • A real WCAG 2.1 AA audit of the actual site — automated scanning tools catch some issues (contrast ratios, missing alt attributes, empty links) but miss others that require a human checking actual keyboard-only navigation and screen-reader behavior on real pages, not just a homepage.
  • Fixing issues in the site's actual code, not layering a widget on top of it — alt text written for real images, semantic HTML headings used in logical order, form fields with proper labels, and focus states visible for keyboard users.
  • Treating this as part of normal site maintenance, not a one-time project — a site that passes an audit today can drift out of compliance as new pages, images, and forms get added without the same standard applied.
  • Documenting the effort — a genuine audit and remediation history is also the practical difference between a business that can show good-faith compliance effort and one that can't, which matters if a claim does arrive regardless of preventive work.

Why this belongs in a web design conversation, not just a legal one

The overlap between "accessible" and "well-built" is large and not a coincidence — proper heading structure, descriptive alt text, keyboard navigability, and sufficient contrast are also simply good, modern web design practice that improves usability for every visitor, not just the subset protected by this specific litigation trend. A site built with real accessibility consideration from the start tends to also be a site with cleaner code, better mobile usability, and often better SEO, since many of the same structural elements (heading hierarchy, descriptive alt text, semantic markup) that accessibility review checks for are also things search engines use to understand a page. This is one of several concrete issues worth checking alongside the broader problems covered in common website mistakes that cost you customers — and it's exactly the kind of thing a real website audit should be checking for, not glossing over.

Where to actually start

Run a real accessibility check against WCAG 2.1 AA on the site's actual, current pages — not just the homepage, and not just an automated scanner's surface-level report. If an overlay widget is currently the site's only accessibility measure, treat that as a starting point to build from, not a finished solution, and prioritize fixing the underlying code issues an overlay can't reach. For a business actively planning a new site or a redesign, building accessibility in from the start — the same way a web design company should be evaluated on more than visual polish — is considerably cheaper and more durable than retrofitting an existing site after the fact.

Frequently asked questions

How many website accessibility lawsuits were filed in 2025?

Seyfarth Shaw's annual tracker, published in its March 2026 report "Federal Court Website Accessibility Lawsuit Filings Bounce Back in 2025," counted 3,117 website accessibility lawsuits filed in federal court in 2025 — a 27% increase over 2024. That figure represented 36% of all 8,667 federal ADA Title III lawsuits filed that year (which itself was down 2% from 2024). Other trackers that also count state-court filings, such as UsableNet, report higher total figures because they're measuring a broader set of courts, not because the underlying trend differs.

What accessibility standard do courts actually use in these cases?

WCAG (Web Content Accessibility Guidelines), most commonly version 2.1 at the AA conformance level, is the standard courts and plaintiffs' attorneys most commonly cite as the yardstick for whether a website is accessible, even though the ADA itself doesn't name a specific technical standard for websites. Meeting WCAG 2.1 AA — covering things like color contrast, keyboard navigation, alt text for images, and proper heading structure — is the most defensible, concrete target for a small business site to aim for.

Does an accessibility overlay widget protect a website from being sued?

Not reliably, based on available reporting. Industry trackers covering 2026 filings report that a meaningful share of sued businesses were already using an accessibility overlay or widget at the time they were sued, since these tools can flag the presence of some accessibility features without actually remediating the underlying code-level barriers plaintiffs' attorneys and automated scanners check for. A genuine code-level audit against WCAG 2.1 AA is a more defensible approach than relying on an overlay alone.
