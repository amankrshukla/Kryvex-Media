Can You Actually Own an AI-Generated Logo or Ad Image? What Firefly, Midjourney, and Copyright Law Say

More small businesses are generating logos, ad creative, and social graphics with AI tools instead of commissioning a designer for every asset — but "I generated it with AI" raises two separate legal questions most people never untangle: does the tool's license let you use the image commercially, and does the law actually protect you from someone else copying it? The answers aren't the same question, and they aren't always the answer businesses assume.

K
Kryvex Media Team
Nov 1, 2026 · 10 min read

There are two entirely different questions bundled into "can I use this AI-generated image for my business," and most of the confusion online comes from answering one while thinking it covers the other. The first is a licensing question: did the AI tool's terms of service grant you the right to use the output commercially? The second is a copyright question: does the law actually protect that image as your intellectual property, the way a traditionally designed logo would be? A small business can clear the first hurdle easily and still be surprised by the second.

Question one: does the tool's license allow commercial use?

This one has clearer, if tool-specific, answers:

  • Adobe Firefly: all paid Adobe Creative Cloud plans include full commercial-use rights for Firefly-generated outputs. Adobe also offers IP indemnification on its paid plans — if a third party sues over an output's alleged infringement, Adobe has stated it will cover the legal defense and resulting damages. That indemnification is specifically reserved for paying subscribers, not free-tier users.
  • Midjourney: commercial usage rights come with any paid subscription. Free trial users have no commercial usage rights at all. Unlike Adobe, Midjourney's terms do not include the same indemnification commitment — if a dispute arises over a Midjourney output, the user carries more of that legal exposure directly.

The practical difference: Firefly's combination of licensed training data and indemnification makes it the more conservative choice for a business that wants to minimize legal risk around what it publishes. Midjourney offers commercial rights too, just without the same backstop if something goes wrong. Either way, terms of service change — check the current version directly before relying on a summary (including this one) for a specific legal decision.

Licensing and copyright are not the same protection. A tool's terms of service can give you permission to use an image — that's a contract between you and the company. Whether the image itself is legally yours in a way that stops someone else from copying it is a separate question governed by copyright law, and AI-generated images sit in genuinely unsettled territory there.

Question two: does copyright actually protect an AI-generated image?

Here's where it gets harder, and where a lot of the plausible-sounding advice online overstates the protection available. The US Copyright Office's position, formalized in a policy statement issued March 16, 2023 and reaffirmed in later guidance, is unambiguous on the core principle: copyright protection requires human authorship. A purely AI-generated image — one where a prompt produced an output with no further meaningful human creative contribution — is not eligible for copyright registration as a standalone work.

The clearest real-world example of this is the Kashtanova case: the Copyright Office reviewed a comic book that used Midjourney-generated images and ultimately cancelled the original registration for those specific images, finding that the AI-generated elements were not Kashtanova's own original authorship. The Office did preserve protection for the parts that were genuinely human-authored — the written text, and the human-made selection, coordination, and arrangement of the images into the finished work. That split is the model worth understanding: the Office doesn't categorically reject every work that touches AI, but it draws a hard line around the specific generated pixels themselves.

A later Copyright Office letter reported that its Registration Division has examined hundreds of submitted works incorporating AI-generated material, and many were rejected for insufficient human authorship or for failing to properly disclose the AI-generated portions on the application. Disclosure isn't optional — applicants are required to identify AI-generated content and describe their own human contribution using the application's "Author Created" field, and the case-by-case review is specifically looking at how much of the final result came from human creative choices versus the AI output alone.

So what does that mean for a business logo made entirely by AI?

Practically: a logo generated purely from a text prompt, used exactly as the AI produced it with no further human editing, likely sits outside copyright protection in the US under this standard. That doesn't make it illegal to use — it means if a competitor or anyone else copies that exact image, your legal footing to stop them on copyright grounds specifically is weaker than it would be for a traditionally designed, human-authored logo. Trademark law is a separate and often more relevant protection for a business logo specifically (trademark protects a mark's role identifying your business in commerce, regardless of how it was created), but that's a different legal framework with its own registration process, not a copyright substitute.

The guidance across multiple sources is consistent: adding meaningful human creative editing on top of an AI output — refining composition, combining and arranging multiple generated elements, hand-adjusting specific details — strengthens a business's position by creating a clearer, documentable human-authorship claim over the final result, even if the Copyright Office's underlying AI-generated elements still can't be protected on their own.

The active litigation worth knowing about

Disney and Universal filed suit against Midjourney in June 2025 over alleged copyright infringement related to how the tool was trained and what it can generate, and as of 2026 there is active litigation involving AI image generators more broadly. None of this directly changes what a small business can do today, but it's a live reason the legal landscape here could shift — a business making heavy, ongoing use of AI-generated visuals in its branding should treat this as a space to keep half an eye on rather than something permanently settled.

A practical checklist before you publish an AI-generated image

  1. Check the specific tool's current commercial-use terms before relying on any summary, including this one — licensing terms for AI tools have changed before and can change again.
  2. Favor tools with indemnification (like Firefly's paid plans) for anything going into paid advertising or permanent branding, where legal exposure matters more than for a one-off social post.
  3. Add real human creative editing on top of any AI output you intend to use as a core brand asset — not just for the stronger legal position, but because hand-refined composition and detail usually produces a better final result than a raw generation anyway.
  4. Consider trademark registration for your actual logo regardless of how it was created — it protects a different, often more relevant interest than copyright does for a business mark.
  5. Document your own creative contribution (prompt iteration choices, manual edits, the arrangement of multiple generated elements into a final composition) if you ever intend to register anything built with AI assistance — you'll need to describe that human contribution on the application.

Where this fits with working alongside a designer rather than instead of one

None of this makes AI generation unusable for a small business — it's a genuinely useful tool for exploring directions quickly, generating supporting visuals, or drafting concepts to react to. The legal nuance above argues for a specific workflow, not against the tool itself: use AI generation as a starting point, then apply real human design judgment on top before anything becomes a permanent brand asset. That's consistent with how we approach graphics design for clients generally — AI-assisted drafting sped up, human-led refinement and final decision-making kept, since the parts of design work that actually need protecting (and that clients actually value) are the human judgment calls, not the raw pixel output.

Frequently asked questions

Can a small business legally use an AI-generated image in its ads or branding?

Generally yes, provided the tool's own license allows commercial use. Adobe Firefly includes full commercial-use rights on all paid Creative Cloud plans, and Midjourney grants commercial rights on paid subscriptions (but not on its free trial). Always check the specific tool's current terms, since licensing structures can change.

Does copyright protect a purely AI-generated logo or image?

Not fully. The US Copyright Office's position, formalized in its March 2023 policy statement and reaffirmed since, is that copyright protection requires human authorship. A purely AI-generated image, with no meaningful human creative input, is not eligible for copyright registration on its own. Human-edited or human-arranged elements added on top of an AI output can be protected.

What's the difference between Adobe Firefly and Midjourney for commercial legal protection?

The key difference reported across multiple sources is indemnification. Adobe offers IP indemnification to paying Firefly subscribers, meaning Adobe covers legal defense and damages if a third party sues over an output's alleged infringement. Midjourney grants commercial usage rights on paid plans but does not offer the same indemnification protection, leaving more of the legal risk with the user.
