Ecommerce Marketing That Actually Converts Browsers Into Buyers

Getting a shopper to the product page is the easy half of ecommerce marketing. The harder, more expensive problem — the one that quietly erases the return on every ad dollar already spent — is what happens between "add to cart" and "order confirmed." Here's where online stores actually lose sales, where the free wins are, and the order to fix it in.

K
Kryvex Media Team
Oct 21, 2026 · 10 min read

Ecommerce marketing is usually described as a traffic problem, and that's only half right. Most online stores can, with enough ad spend, get a reasonable number of people to a product page. What separates the stores that actually grow from the ones that burn budget is what happens after that click — whether the product is actually findable for free in the first place, whether the checkout survives contact with a real shopper, and whether the people who don't buy the first time ever hear from the store again. None of that is exotic. It's a short list of specific mechanics, most of them under-used even by stores spending real money on ads.

The checkout is where most of the revenue actually dies

Before talking about how to get more traffic, it's worth being honest about where existing traffic already goes. Industry research that aggregates dozens of checkout studies — most frequently attributed to the Baymard Institute's long-running meta-analysis — has put the average documented online shopping cart abandonment rate at roughly 70% for close to a decade, with the figure consistently sitting in a fairly narrow band year over year. That means, on average, for every ten shoppers who add something to a cart, around seven leave without buying. This is the single most expensive problem in ecommerce marketing, and it's almost entirely a product, pricing-transparency and checkout-design problem rather than a traffic or ad-targeting one — which is exactly why it's so often ignored in favor of spending more on acquisition instead of fixing the leak that's already there.

The reasons behind that abandonment are consistently named across this research, even though the exact share attributed to each one varies by study and year. The most consistently cited reason is unexpected extra costs surfacing late in checkout — shipping, tax, or fees that weren't visible earlier in the process, which reads to a shopper as a bait-and-switch even when it isn't intended as one. Being forced to create an account before completing a purchase is also named repeatedly as a major abandonment driver, which is why offering a genuine guest-checkout option is one of the highest-leverage, lowest-cost fixes a store can make. A checkout that's slow, has too many steps, or doesn't clearly show a running total before the final page rounds out the usual list. None of these require a redesign — they require an honest walk through the actual checkout flow, on a phone, as if buying something for the first time.

Recovering a fraction of a roughly-70%-abandonment problem is almost always cheaper than acquiring the equivalent number of new visitors through paid ads — which is why the checkout and the abandoned-cart email flow deserve attention before the next ad budget increase, not after.

Getting found for free: Google's product listings

Before paying for a single click, every store with a product feed should make sure it's eligible for Google's free listings. Per Google's own Merchant Center documentation, these listings can surface a store's products at no cost across Google Search, the Shopping tab, Google Maps, Google Images, Google Lens and YouTube, and they're typically switched on by default once a product feed is connected. Stores on Shopify, WooCommerce, PrestaShop or similar platforms can usually connect this feed automatically rather than building one by hand. The catch isn't cost, it's eligibility — Google is explicit that turning the setting on doesn't guarantee any specific product will actually be shown, since matching depends entirely on how complete and accurate the submitted product data is (titles, images, price, availability, and the right product category). A thin or outdated feed quietly forfeits free visibility that a complete one would have earned. When a shopper does click a free listing, Google sends them straight to that product's page on the store's own site, which means the product page itself still has to do the actual selling.

Paid acquisition: Shopping ads and social commerce aren't interchangeable

Google Shopping campaigns and Performance Max put products in front of people who are already searching for that product or something close to it — high-intent traffic, closer to a purchase decision, running on the same product feed that powers the free listings. Meta and Instagram Shopping ads work differently: they're built for visual discovery, introducing a product to someone who wasn't already looking for it, using the kind of scroll-stopping imagery that search ads can't replicate. Treating these as competing budgets to choose between, rather than two different jobs in the funnel, is a common and costly mistake — Shopping ads harvest demand that already exists, while Meta and Instagram ads create demand that didn't exist yet. A store relying only on one is typically either overpaying to compete for the same high-intent searches everyone else is bidding on, or generating awareness with no efficient way to capture the resulting demand when it search-returns later. Our PPC team and Meta Ads team usually run both in parallel for ecommerce clients for exactly this reason.

The email flows that recover revenue that's already paid for

Every visitor an ecommerce store gets through paid or organic traffic has already been paid for, in ad spend or in time. Email is the channel that turns that one-time visit into a second, third and tenth one, and a handful of automated flows do almost all of the real work — not one-off promotional blasts. The abandoned-cart flow is the single highest-priority build, because it's reaching people who already decided to buy and simply didn't finish; triggered a short while after a cart is left, often in two or three messages rather than one, it's recovering demand that's already been generated rather than trying to create new demand from scratch. A welcome series for new subscribers (who typically haven't bought yet) and a post-purchase flow — order confirmation, shipping updates, and a well-timed review request once the product has actually arrived — round out the next two highest-leverage builds. A browse-abandonment flow (triggered by product views without an add-to-cart) and a win-back flow for lapsed past customers extend the system further once the first three are solid. These are the same category of automated sequence covered in our broader email flows guide, but for an ecommerce store specifically, abandoned-cart and post-purchase carry outsized weight because they're acting directly on the checkout-abandonment problem described above.

SEO and content: the compounding channel most stores under-invest in

Paid acquisition and email both have a ceiling tied to budget or existing list size; organic search doesn't have the same ceiling, which is exactly why it's worth building even though it's slower to pay off. Category and collection pages that are genuinely optimized — clear titles, useful on-page copy beyond just a product grid, and a sensible internal linking structure — are what let a store rank for the broader, higher-volume searches around a product type, not just branded searches for the store's own name. Buying guides, comparison content and genuine how-to content built around real customer questions (sizing, compatibility, care instructions, "X vs Y") give a store something to rank for before a shopper has decided exactly what to buy, which is earlier and often cheaper to capture than the bottom-of-funnel searches everyone is bidding on in Shopping and search ads. Genuine customer reviews and user-generated photos on product pages do double duty — they're a trust signal for the shopper deciding whether to buy, and they're fresh, unique content that a thin, manufacturer-supplied product description on its own doesn't provide. Our SEO team treats ecommerce category and content SEO as a distinct discipline from local SEO for exactly this reason — the intent being targeted and the page types involved are different.

The mistakes that quietly cap ecommerce revenue

  • No guest checkout, or an account wall before purchase. One of the most consistently named abandonment drivers across checkout research, and one of the cheapest to fix.
  • Shipping and fees revealed only at the final step. Showing the full cost earlier in the flow removes the single most commonly cited reason shoppers abandon a cart they'd already filled.
  • An incomplete or stale Google product feed. Free visibility across Search, Images, Maps and the Shopping tab is being left on the table for something that costs nothing but data-feed maintenance.
  • Running only Shopping ads or only social ads, not both. Each channel is solving a different half of the demand funnel; running one alone caps the other half's potential.
  • No abandoned-cart email flow. This is reaching the store's highest-intent, already-warmed audience and, if it doesn't exist, that recoverable revenue is simply never recovered.

Where to actually start

In rough priority order, since fixing all of this at once isn't realistic: audit the checkout first for the three named abandonment drivers above (guest checkout, upfront cost transparency, step count) because it affects every visitor the store already has, regardless of channel; build or fix the abandoned-cart email flow second, since it's acting directly on that same leak; make sure the Google product feed is complete and the free listings are actually eligible third, because it costs nothing and compounds; then layer in or rebalance Shopping versus social ad spend fourth; and treat category-page and content SEO as the fifth, longer-horizon build that keeps paying off after the others are in place. Most stores jump straight to step four — spending more on ads — without ever doing the first three, which is the single most common reason a growing ad budget doesn't translate into proportionally growing revenue. That diagnostic work across checkout, feed, email and content is what our ecommerce marketing team runs for every new client before recommending any change to ad spend.

Frequently asked questions

What's the single biggest reason ecommerce stores lose sales they already won?

Checkout abandonment. Industry research that aggregates dozens of studies on this (most frequently attributed to the Baymard Institute's meta-analysis) has put the average documented online shopping cart abandonment rate at roughly 70% for close to a decade. That means the majority of shoppers who already added something to a cart still leave without buying — a problem that sits entirely after the ad spend has already done its job.

Are Google's free product listings actually free, or is there a catch?

They're genuinely free. Per Google's own Merchant Center documentation, free listings can surface a store's products across Google Search, the Shopping tab, Google Images, Google Maps and Google Lens at no cost, usually turned on by default once a product feed is connected. The catch is eligibility, not cost: Google doesn't guarantee any specific product will actually be shown, since matching depends on how complete and accurate the submitted product data is.

Which email flow should an ecommerce store build first?

The abandoned-cart flow, because it's reaching people who already decided to buy and just didn't finish — the highest-intent audience a store has. A welcome series for new subscribers and a post-purchase flow (order confirmation, shipping updates, a review request) are the next two highest-leverage builds, in that order.

Is it worth running both Google Shopping ads and Meta/Instagram ads for an online store?

For most stores, yes, because they catch shoppers at different points. Google Shopping and Performance Max campaigns reach people actively searching for a product, which is typically the higher-intent, closer-to-purchase moment. Meta and Instagram Shopping ads are better at creating demand with people who weren't already searching, using visual product discovery to introduce the product in the first place. Running only one usually means missing one half of the funnel.
