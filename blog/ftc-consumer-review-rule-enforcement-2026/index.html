The FTC's Fake Review Rule Has Teeth Now: What Small Businesses Need to Know

The FTC's Rule on Reviews and Testimonials has been on the books since October 2024, but 2026 is the year it stopped being a quiet compliance footnote. December 2025 brought the agency's first public warning letters under the rule. By May 2026, the FTC and the Illinois Attorney General had filed an actual lawsuit over fake reviews. Per the FTC's own warning letter template, penalties now run up to $53,088 per violation, and the rule carries no small business exemption.

If your business, or anyone you've hired to manage your online reputation, has ever leaned on a staff member's family writing a glowing review, a vendor offering a discount in exchange for five stars, or an SEO firm quietly posting reviews on your behalf, the legal ground under that practice shifted meaningfully in 2026. The FTC's Rule on Reviews and Testimonials, officially 16 CFR Part 465, has existed since it took effect on October 21, 2024. What changed is enforcement, and the shift from quiet guidance to real cases happened fast enough that a lot of small businesses and the agencies that serve them haven't caught up to what's actually at stake.

From warning letters to an actual lawsuit in five months

The FTC's December 2025 warning letters mark the first time the agency publicly enforced this specific rule rather than simply publishing guidance about it. Those letters demanded prompt corrective action and signaled, according to legal commentary covering the shift, that the agency was moving from an education posture to active enforcement, with civil penalties genuinely on the table rather than theoretical. The FTC followed through quickly. In May 2026, the agency joined the Illinois Attorney General in suing a company called Premium Home Service, alleging thousands of fake business listings and fake five star reviews posted by employees, relatives, and search engine optimization firms working on the company's behalf. That detail, SEO firms specifically named as part of the alleged scheme, is worth sitting with if your own review strategy has ever involved outside help you haven't closely audited.

The liability question that catches businesses off guard

The part of this rule that trips people up most isn't the existence of the rule itself. It's who actually bears responsibility when something goes wrong. Legal analysis of the rule's liability standard is consistent on this point: delegating review management to a third party vendor does not shift liability away from the business whose name sits on the listing. Whether a business is on the hook depends on whether it knew, or reasonably should have known, that reviews were fake, incentivized without disclosure, or otherwise manipulated. Hiring an agency to "handle reviews" and never asking how doesn't create a shield. If anything, a business that never checks on the methods behind its own glowing review count is in a weaker position to argue it didn't know, not a stronger one.

Per the FTC's own December 2025 warning letter template, a federal court could impose civil penalties of up to $53,088 per violation. That figure is adjusted annually for inflation under federal law; the rule's original 2024 publication set the starting maximum at $51,744 per violation. The exact current number is worth confirming directly against 16 CFR 1.98 before you rely on it for anything beyond a general sense of scale, since it is set to rise again as the annual adjustment takes effect.

No small business exemption, but enforcement has a pattern so far

The rule itself contains nothing that carves out small businesses from its reach. That said, it's fair and accurate to note a real pattern in how enforcement has actually unfolded: the FTC's highest profile 2026 case targeted a larger company with an alleged pattern of thousands of fake listings and reviews, not a single local business with a handful of questionable reviews. Legal commentary on the rule explicitly flags that single instance violations by small businesses haven't been the dominant enforcement target to date. That's a genuine, documented fact about enforcement priorities so far in 2026, not a promise about what happens next, and it would be a mistake to read it as a guarantee that small operators are safe. Enforcement priorities can and do shift, and the rule gives the FTC the legal basis to act against any business, of any size, the moment it decides to.

What this actually covers, beyond the obvious case

The rule's scope goes beyond the clearest example of a business simply inventing five star reviews from nowhere. It also reaches review gating, where a business filters which customers get asked to leave a public review based on how satisfied they seemed first. It covers undisclosed incentives, meaning any discount, free product, or payment offered in exchange for a review, if that incentive isn't clearly disclosed. It covers insider reviews, where an employee, owner, or close relative posts a review without disclosing that connection, even if every word of the review is sincere. A business can run afoul of this rule without ever writing a single fabricated sentence, simply by managing who gets asked to review and how that process is incentivized.

Why this matters more for local and service businesses specifically

Reviews carry disproportionate weight for exactly the kind of businesses that spend the most on local marketing: clinics, contractors, restaurants, salons, and other service providers where a prospective customer is choosing between several nearby options and leaning heavily on star ratings and recent review text to decide. That same weight is precisely what makes review manipulation tempting in the first place, and it's also what makes a formal FTC complaint, or even just a warning letter becoming public, so damaging beyond the fine itself. A business named in an enforcement action over fake reviews doesn't just face a potential penalty. It faces the exact reputational harm the fake reviews were meant to prevent, except now attached to a federal case rather than a few unhappy customers.

What to actually do about this

If an agency or SEO firm manages your reviews, ask specifically how. Don't accept a vague answer about "boosting your online reputation." Ask whether any reviews are incentivized, who is being asked to leave them, and whether any connection to your business (employee, relative, or vendor) is disclosed where required.

Stop any review gating practice today if you're running one. Asking only satisfied looking customers to leave a public review while quietly routing unhappy ones to a private feedback form is squarely inside what this rule targets, even if every review collected is genuinely from a real customer.

Disclose any incentive clearly, every time, not occasionally. If you offer a discount or free item for leaving a review, that connection needs to be stated plainly alongside the review itself, not buried in terms nobody reads.

Treat "we didn't know what our vendor was doing" as no defense at all. Given how liability actually works under this rule, that's not a safety net. It's closer to an admission that nobody was checking.

Frequently asked questions

When did the FTC's review rule actually take effect?

The FTC's Rule on Reviews and Testimonials, officially 16 CFR Part 465, took effect on October 21, 2024. What changed in 2025 and 2026 wasn't the rule itself, but enforcement. The FTC issued its first public warning letters under the rule in December 2025, and by May 2026 had moved to an actual lawsuit, filed jointly with the Illinois Attorney General, against a company called Premium Home Service.

If I pay a marketing agency or SEO company to handle my reviews, am I protected if they do something that violates the rule?

No. Per legal analysis of the rule's liability standard, delegating review management to a third party vendor does not shift liability away from the business whose name and listing the reviews are attached to. Liability under the rule depends on whether the business knew or should have known the reviews were fake or manipulated, not on who physically posted them. Outsourcing the task doesn't outsource the legal responsibility.

Is there a small business exemption from this rule?

No. The rule itself contains no small business carve out. Legal commentary on the rule notes that single instance violations by small businesses have not yet been the dominant target of FTC enforcement actions so far, with the agency's highest profile 2026 action aimed at a larger company. That is a pattern in how enforcement has been prioritized to date, not a legal exemption, and it is not something a business should rely on as protection.
