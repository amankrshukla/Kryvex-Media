TikTok's US Ownership Deal: What It Actually Means for Small Business Advertisers

After more than two years of uncertainty, TikTok's US ownership question has an actual answer: a new joint venture closed its deal on January 22, 2026. But "ownership changed" and "advertising changed" are two different questions, and the reporting on this deal draws a clear, often-missed line between them. Here's what the deal structure actually says, who still runs the ads business, and what a small business advertiser should realistically do with that information.

K
Kryvex Media Team
Nov 4, 2026 · 10 min read

For more than two years, "will TikTok even be here next quarter" was a real planning question for US advertisers, not a hypothetical one. That question now has a concrete answer, even if several follow-up questions don't yet. On January 22, 2026, reporting confirmed that TikTok's US ownership restructuring had closed, forming a new entity — TikTok USDS Joint Venture LLC — built under a September 25, 2025 executive order. The deal itself was years in the making, and the details of who owns what, who controls the algorithm, and critically, who actually runs advertising going forward are worth separating out carefully, because several of them are not the same thing marketing commentary sometimes implies.

What actually closed, and who owns what

The ownership structure reported across multiple outlets breaks down roughly as follows: Oracle, Silver Lake, and MGX (an Abu Dhabi-based investment vehicle) each hold approximately 15% of the new joint venture, a combined stake of around 45%. Existing ByteDance investors — separate from ByteDance the company — are reported to hold roughly 30.1%, while ByteDance itself retains a 19.9% stake, which sources describe as the maximum allowed under current US foreign-ownership restrictions for this kind of structure. A reported valuation of roughly $14 billion for the joint venture has circulated widely, but that figure is explicitly unconfirmed by any primary source in the reporting reviewed for this piece, so treat it as a reported estimate rather than a disclosed fact.

On the technology side, the arrangement is more nuanced than a simple ownership transfer. ByteDance retains ownership of the underlying recommendation algorithm and licenses it to the new joint venture, which is then responsible for retraining that algorithm specifically on US user data. Oracle is reported to host both the data and the algorithm itself within its US cloud infrastructure — a continuation of the "Project Texas" data-localization arrangement Oracle had already been running for TikTok's US data since 2022. One unconfirmed detail worth flagging rather than ignoring: a Bloomberg report, relayed secondhand, suggested the joint venture may pay ByteDance a licensing fee of up to half of its US profits. That figure has not been independently confirmed and should be read as a reported claim, not an established fact.

The part advertisers actually need to know: who runs the ads business

This is the detail that gets lost in a lot of the ownership-deal coverage, and it's the one that matters most for a marketer deciding how to plan Q1 2027 budgets. Multiple reports on the deal structure are explicit that advertising, e-commerce, and marketing operations are not run by the new joint venture itself. Those commercial functions are described as remaining with TikTok's existing global organization and its US entities — the same structure that has run TikTok Ads Manager, Shop, and the Business Help Center all along. eMarketer's own reading of the deal reinforces this: its analysis states that ByteDance continues to be involved in global ecommerce, marketing, and advertising operations, separate from the joint venture that now controls the app's US ownership, data, and algorithm licensing. In plain terms: the entity that changed hands on January 22 is not the same entity that decides your CPM, approves your ad account, or runs the Shop checkout flow.

That distinction matters because a lot of the anxiety driving marketer behavior through 2025 and into 2026 was about continuity of the ad platform itself, not about who held equity in a holding company. Digiday's ongoing coverage through the back half of 2025 described marketers "bracing for TikTok whiplash" after watching a comparable ownership saga play out at X (formerly Twitter), and separately reported that the prolonged uncertainty was causing marketers to actively rethink 2026 budget allocations rather than commit early. eMarketer's own headline on the closed deal struck a deliberately qualified tone — describing it as giving advertisers "greater certainty, though questions remain" — which is a more accurate summary of where things stand than either "nothing changes" or "everything changes."

How big is US advertising on TikTok, and what's actually at stake

The scale here is worth stating plainly, because it explains why this deal got the scrutiny it did. eMarketer figures put US advertising at roughly 47% of TikTok's total 2024 global ad revenue — about $12.29 billion. eMarketer's own September 2025 forecast projected US TikTok ad revenue reaching $17.17 billion in 2026. Put simply: the US is not a side market for TikTok's ad business, it's close to half of it, which is exactly why the platform has every commercial incentive to keep the advertiser experience stable through this transition rather than disrupt it.

That doesn't mean there's zero operational risk. MediaPost's coverage of the joint-venture agreement specifically flagged possible friction points as the new structure settles in — tighter audience-targeting approval processes, potential shifts in content moderation posture, and knock-on effects for brand safety are all mentioned as open questions rather than settled outcomes. The algorithm retraining process itself is the more concrete near-term variable: a recommendation system being retrained specifically on US data, separate from the global model it was previously part of, can plausibly shift how content and ads get distributed during the transition, which in turn can move CPMs and ad performance in either direction for a period of time. eMarketer's analysis frames this explicitly as a transition-period risk rather than a reason to expect a permanent degradation in ad performance.

No source reviewed for this piece confirms that TikTok's small-business ad tools or pricing — Spark Ads, the Small Business resource center, or self-serve campaign minimums — have actually changed as a direct result of the ownership deal. If you see a claim that SMB ad programs were "overhauled" because of the joint venture, that's getting ahead of what's actually been confirmed; treat it skeptically until TikTok's own Business Help Center documentation says otherwise.

What a small business should actually do with this information

The honest, non-alarmist read is this: the biggest source of existential uncertainty — "will the app still exist in the US a year from now" — has a real, closed answer now, and that's genuinely useful for budget planning in a way it wasn't through most of 2025. But the ownership change is not, by itself, a signal to dramatically increase or decrease ad spend. The more useful signal is operational: watch your own account's performance metrics — CPM, CTR, conversion rate — over the next one to two reporting cycles rather than reacting to deal-closing headlines alone. If the algorithm retraining process does cause temporary performance swings, they'll show up in your own dashboard before they show up in any trade-press follow-up piece.

Practically, that means: don't pause a working TikTok campaign purely because of the ownership news, but do build in a short review checkpoint — two to three weeks out — specifically to check whether your cost-per-result has moved meaningfully from its pre-deal baseline. If you've been holding off testing TikTok ads specifically because of the "will it even survive" uncertainty, that specific objection is now weaker than it was; the platform-survival risk and the advertising-continuity risk are different risks, and the deal that closed on January 22 resolves the first one without necessarily resolving the second. For a small business still deciding whether TikTok deserves a place in the paid-social mix at all, that's a meaningfully different starting point than it was six months ago — a reason to test with a modest, bounded budget and real tracking in place, not a reason to assume stability has fully returned to the ad product itself.

Frequently asked questions

Who owns TikTok in the US now?

TikTok USDS Joint Venture LLC, formed under a September 25, 2025 executive order, closed its ownership deal on January 22, 2026. Reporting describes Oracle, Silver Lake, and MGX each holding roughly 15% (about 45% combined), existing ByteDance investors holding around 30.1%, and ByteDance itself retaining a 19.9% stake, the cap allowed under US foreign-ownership rules.

Does the new joint venture run TikTok's advertising?

No. Reporting on the deal structure says advertising, e-commerce, and marketing are not run by the joint venture itself; those commercial functions are described as staying with TikTok's existing global organization and its US entities, with ByteDance continuing to be involved in global ecommerce, marketing, and advertising operations per eMarketer's reading of the deal.

Should small businesses change their TikTok ad budgets because of the deal?

There's no confirmed evidence that ad tools, pricing, or small-business programs have changed because of the ownership deal itself. The more realistic near-term risk, flagged by industry commentary, is a transition period as the recommendation algorithm is retrained on US data, which could affect CPMs and ad performance temporarily — reason enough to test cautiously and watch results closely rather than overhaul a budget based on the ownership change alone.
