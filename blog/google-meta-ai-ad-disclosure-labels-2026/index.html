Google and Meta Now Require AI Disclosure on Ad Creative — What Changed in 2026

Through the middle of 2026, Google and Meta both rolled out new requirements to disclose when ad creative was made or edited with AI, and the EU AI Act's own transparency rules took effect on top of that in August. None of this is a single unified rule — it's three overlapping sets of requirements from three different sources, each with its own scope, triggers, and timing. Here's what each one actually requires, sourced from the platforms' own policy pages and multiple independent reports, and what it means for a small business running ads today.

Nov 3, 2026 · 10 min read

"Make sure the AI disclosure is right" quietly became a real item on the pre-launch checklist for digital ads in 2026. Three separate developments converged within a few weeks of each other: Google rolled out mandatory AI-labeling requirements for ad creative, Meta introduced its own disclosure framework for Facebook and Instagram ads, and the EU AI Act's transparency obligations became legally applicable across the European Union. None of the three rules is identical to the others, and none of them is a simple "AI ads are banned" policy — all three permit AI-assisted creative, they just require telling someone (the platform, the viewer, or both) that AI was involved. Getting the details right matters, because the requirements, triggers, and penalties genuinely differ by platform and by jurisdiction.

Google: mandatory labeling since July 2026

Google's own support documentation, titled "Updates to AI labeling requirements (July 2026)," describes a disclosure requirement that rolled out across Google's ad surfaces in July 2026 — secondary coverage reports the effective date as either July 9 or July 11, 2026, so treat the exact day as approximate pending direct confirmation from Google's page. The mechanism splits into two paths depending on which tool made the creative. Ads built using Google's own AI creative tools get labeled automatically — no advertiser action required. Ads that use AI-generated or AI-edited assets from a third-party tool (Canva is the example most commonly cited in coverage of the rule) require the advertiser to manually disclose that in Google's Asset Studio; Google doesn't detect third-party AI use on its own.

The visible result for a consumer is a "How this ad was made" panel inside Google's My Ad Center, where a user can see whether and how AI was involved in a given ad's creative. Coverage of the rule also notes that in jurisdictions with their own disclosure mandates — the EU, India, and New York are cited in reporting — the label can additionally appear directly on the ad itself, not just inside the transparency panel. What the rule doesn't do is create an entirely new violation category: deciding whether a given asset counts as "AI-generated" enough to require disclosure is left to the advertiser, and getting that judgment wrong doesn't automatically trigger a penalty on its own — though it can compound an existing deceptive-ads violation if the ad is also misleading in substance, not just in how it discloses AI use. Google's underlying policy against misleading advertising is otherwise unchanged; this rule is about disclosure, not about restricting what AI can be used to make.

Meta: a tiered label depending on how much AI was used

Meta's approach, rolled out across Facebook and Instagram around the same mid-2026 window, scales the visibility of the disclosure to how significant the AI involvement is. According to 2026 reporting on the rollout, a lower-visibility, general AI-assistance disclosure can sit inside an ad's three-dot options menu — available to anyone who looks for it, but not surfaced automatically. Ads that contain AI-generated photorealistic depictions of human beings get a more prominent treatment: the label appears directly next to the ad's "Sponsored" tag, where it's far more likely to actually be seen by the person viewing the ad. That distinction — menu-level disclosure for general AI assistance, prominent placement specifically for synthetic human imagery — is the core design choice in Meta's framework, and it reflects where deception risk is highest: a realistic AI-generated person in an ad carries more potential to mislead than, say, an AI-assisted background edit. Meta has described the rollout itself as gradual, and some reporting suggests the label isn't yet showing on every eligible ad, so advertisers shouldn't assume the absence of a visible label on a given ad reflects settled, final behavior.

The EU AI Act: a legal obligation, not a platform policy

The EU AI Act's transparency rules are a different kind of requirement entirely — not a platform's internal policy but a binding law, with real penalties attached. Article 50 of the Act's transparency obligations became applicable on August 2, 2026; that date is corroborated independently across several law firms' own client trackers (Cooley, Sidley, and BSI Group all cite it, among others), which gives it more confidence than a single-source claim. Several of those same trackers note that generative AI systems already on the market before August 2, 2026 get a grace period until December 2, 2026 specifically to meet the machine-readable marking duty for synthetic content under Article 50(2) — the requirement that AI-generated or AI-manipulated content be marked in a way machines can detect, not just a visible label for humans. Penalties for non-compliance are reported at up to €15 million or 3% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher, which is a materially different risk profile from a platform simply rejecting or re-labeling a non-compliant ad. The Act's Digital Omnibus process reportedly deferred some separate, higher-risk AI obligations to December 2027, but left the Article 50 transparency timeline itself unchanged.

It's worth being clear about scope: the Act applies based on where the content reaches EU users and who the "provider" or "deployer" is under the regulation, not simply based on where a business is headquartered. A small business outside the EU running ads that reach EU audiences should treat this as a real compliance question worth a few minutes of research, not something that only applies to companies physically located in Europe.

The U.S. Federal Trade Commission, by contrast, hasn't issued a dedicated new rule for AI ad disclosure as of this writing. The FTC's established approach applies its existing truth-in-advertising and endorsement guidance to AI-generated content rather than creating an AI-specific regulation — meaning a deceptive AI-made ad is still enforceable under the same deception standards that apply to any other misleading advertising, AI-made or not.

What this actually means for a small business running ads

None of these three rules asks a business to stop using AI in ad creative. Each one asks for honesty about when AI was used, scaled to how much that use could mislead someone. In practice, that breaks into a short, concrete checklist: know which creative tool actually made each asset (Google's own tools self-disclose; anything else needs a manual flag in Asset Studio); treat any AI-generated photorealistic person in a Meta ad as a higher-scrutiny asset that will likely carry a prominent label, not a quiet one; and if campaigns run in or reach the EU, build a few minutes of Article 50 awareness into the creative-review process rather than treating it as someone else's problem. None of this requires abandoning AI-assisted creative tools — graphic design and ad-creative workflows increasingly use them productively — it just requires treating the disclosure step as a real, non-optional part of launching a campaign rather than an afterthought.
