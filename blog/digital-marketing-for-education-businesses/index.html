Digital Marketing for Education Businesses: What FERPA and COPPA Actually Restrict

Schools, tutoring centers, test-prep businesses and edtech platforms all compete for enrollments the same way any local business competes for customers — except the data behind that marketing is regulated in ways a retailer or restaurant never has to think about. FERPA, COPPA and a growing list of state student-privacy laws put real limits on how student names, photos and behavioral data can be used in advertising. None of that means an education business should market less — it means knowing exactly where the lines sit before a testimonial, a retargeting pixel or a vendor contract crosses one.

"Education business" covers a wide range of marketers under one roof — a K-12 school, a private tutoring center, a test-prep brand, an online course platform, an edtech app — and the privacy rules attached to each one depend on exactly what it is and who it markets to, not on the fact that it serves students. A school or any agency that receives US Department of Education funding is bound directly by the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA). A private tutoring company with no federal funding isn't bound by FERPA on its own, but it almost always ends up with FERPA-equivalent obligations the moment it signs a vendor contract with a school. And any business, school-affiliated or not, that collects data from users under 13 runs into the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA) — which the FTC materially updated in 2025. Layered on top of both are state laws, some of which go further than federal law does. The mechanics of winning students — a strong Google presence, targeted ads, content that answers real parent and student search intent — work the same way they do for any local business. What's different is the real compliance layer sitting underneath, covered below with the actual rules and the real 2025-2026 changes to them.

FERPA and directory information: the rule that governs testimonials and enrollment marketing

FERPA (20 U.S.C. § 1232g; implementing regulations at 34 CFR Part 99) protects the privacy of student education records and applies to educational agencies and institutions that receive funding under a program administered by the US Department of Education — which covers public K-12 districts and the vast majority of colleges and universities, but not a private tutoring company operating independently of any school. The provision that actually touches marketing is the "directory information" exception at 34 CFR 99.37: a school may disclose directory information — commonly a student's name, photo, grade level, dates of attendance or honors — without separate written consent, but only if it has first given public notice of exactly which categories count as directory information at that school, and given parents and eligible students a real window to opt out before any such disclosure. Once a parent or eligible student files that opt-out, the school has to honor it going forward, including for former students, unless the opt-out is later rescinded. The regulation also lets a school narrow who it shares directory information with under 34 CFR 99.37(d) — for example, limiting it to internal uses and excluding outside marketing vendors entirely — and that narrower policy has to be reflected in the same public notice.

What this means in practice for a school's own marketing team, or an outside agency working with one: before a brochure, a recruitment video or an enrollment-campaign landing page uses a specific student's name or photo, confirm (1) that the school's directory-information notice actually covers that category of information, and (2) that the specific student hasn't filed an opt-out. Neither step is optional, and neither is a one-time check — opt-out lists change every enrollment cycle. A private tutoring or test-prep business that isn't itself bound by FERPA still needs the family's own direct consent before using a minor's name or likeness in an ad, which is a lower bar procedurally but not one to skip — children's names, photos and school affiliations are exactly the kind of personal information parents expect a say over, regardless of which specific statute technically applies.

If an education business works with schools on co-marketed enrollment campaigns, ask for the school's current directory-information notice and its current opt-out list before every campaign, not once at the start of the relationship. Directory-information policies and opt-out rosters are both allowed to change, and 34 CFR 99.37 puts the burden of honoring an opt-out on whoever is disclosing the information.

COPPA in 2026: a real rule change that affects retargeting and ad tech, not just sign-up forms

COPPA and its implementing rule (16 CFR Part 312) apply to any operator of a commercial website or online service directed to children under 13, and separately to any operator with actual knowledge it is collecting personal information from a child under 13 — including knowledge gained because another child-directed service told it so. For any education business whose users plausibly include under-13 students — an elementary tutoring brand, a kids' coding platform, a school's own under-13 portal — this is not a theoretical rule. The FTC finalized its first major update to the COPPA Rule since 2013 in the Federal Register on April 22, 2025, with the amended rule taking effect June 23, 2025 and a compliance deadline of April 22, 2026. The marketing-relevant change: operators must now get separate, specific opt-in parental consent before using a child's personal information for targeted advertising, and that consent cannot be bundled into a general data-collection consent — it has to be its own, specific ask. Operators also have to tell parents when agreeing to third-party data sharing is optional, unless that sharing is integral to the service itself. Notably, the FTC's 2025 update left out a proposed edtech-specific carve-out that would have let schools authorize consent on parents' behalf more broadly; the Commission said it held that provision back specifically to avoid conflicting with the Department of Education's own ongoing work on FERPA, and said it would keep enforcing COPPA in edtech under its existing guidance in the meantime.

That existing guidance is the FTC's Policy Statement on Education Technology, adopted unanimously on May 19, 2022. It doesn't create new legal requirements, but it tells ed tech providers exactly how the Commission reads COPPA's existing requirements against them: a provider can't condition access to an educational activity on a child supplying more personal information than the activity actually requires (the FTC's own example is requiring a student email address when the provider has no actual need to email students); a provider relying on a school's consent on parents' behalf may use the resulting data only to provide the service the school authorized, not for the provider's own marketing, advertising or other commercial purposes; data retention has to be tied to the service's actual purpose rather than held indefinitely; and reasonable data security is required regardless of whether a breach has actually occurred. For an education marketing team, the direct takeaway is that any student data a school hands over for a legitimate service purpose is off-limits for that provider's own advertising — remarketing lists, lookalike audiences and ad-platform pixels all need their own, separately-consented data source.

State laws that go further than federal law: SOPIPA and New York's Education Law 2-d

Federal law is the floor, not the ceiling, and two state laws matter enough to name specifically. California's Student Online Personal Information Protection Act (SOPIPA, Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 22584, effective January 1, 2016) covers any operator of a site, service or app that the operator knows is used primarily for K-12 school purposes and was designed and marketed for that purpose — no contract with a school is required for the law to apply. SOPIPA bars a covered operator from running targeted advertising on its own service, and separately bars it from using information obtained through that service — including persistent unique identifiers — to target ads anywhere else, including to the students' parents or guardians. It also bars building non-educational student profiles and selling student information outright. New York's Education Law § 2-d and its implementing regulations work from the school's side of the same relationship: NYSED guidance states plainly that the law and its regulations together prohibit educational agencies and their third-party contractors from disclosing student data for commercial or marketing purposes, and the regulations specifically define "commercial or marketing purpose" to include selling student data, using or disclosing it for any form of remuneration, using it for advertising, or using it to develop, improve or market products or services to students — with a narrow exception when a parent or eligible student directly requests a specific product and expressly consents to that specific use. Any education business operating in California or New York, or taking on students who are, needs its vendor contracts and its own ad-tech setup to reflect whichever of these is stricter than COPPA's federal floor.

Where the marketing mechanics are the same as any other local business

Once the directory-information, COPPA-consent and state-law rules above are built into the workflow, the rest of an education business's growth runs on the same fundamentals as any other local service business. A complete, accurately categorized Google Business Profile — correct program types, real hours, genuine parent and student reviews — remains a free, high-leverage starting point, the same one our local SEO team builds for every local client. Paid search continues to work well for high-intent enrollment queries ("tutoring near me," a specific exam-prep course, a specific grade level), as long as landing pages and ad creative are built around data the business can actually show it has consent to use. Content marketing — real, specific guides on test formats, admissions timelines or learning milestones — is one of the lowest-risk, highest-compounding channels available precisely because it doesn't require any individual student's data at all. And email marketing to parents who've opted in directly is a channel the privacy rules above barely touch, since it runs on the parent's own consent rather than data pulled from a school relationship.

Mistakes that create real compliance exposure, not just missed opportunity

  • Using a student's name or photo in an enrollment campaign without checking the school's current directory-information notice and opt-out list. 34 CFR 99.37 puts the burden on whoever discloses the information, and opt-out lists change every term.
  • Running retargeting pixels, ad-network tags or lookalike-audience builders on pages serving users known or likely to be under 13 without the separate, specific opt-in consent the 2025 COPPA amendments require for targeted advertising.
  • Repurposing school-authorized student data for the business's own marketing. The FTC's 2022 Ed Tech Policy Statement treats this as a direct COPPA problem when the data was collected under a school's consent for a service purpose, not a marketing one.
  • Assuming a vendor contract with a California or New York school automatically covers compliance everywhere. SOPIPA's advertising ban and Education Law 2-d's marketing-purpose ban are both state-specific and often stricter than the federal baseline.
  • Treating a private tutoring or edtech business as exempt from all of this because it isn't itself a school. It may be exempt from FERPA directly, but COPPA, the 2022 FTC policy statement and state laws like SOPIPA can still apply based on who it markets to and what data it collects — not on its federal-funding status.

Where to actually start

In order: pull the current directory-information notice and opt-out roster for every school partner before the next campaign that could use a student's name or photo; confirm whether any marketing channel (retargeting, lookalike audiences, ad-platform pixels) touches data from users who are or might be under 13, and get the separate opt-in consent the 2025 COPPA amendments require before that channel goes live, ahead of the April 22, 2026 compliance deadline; audit vendor contracts with California and New York schools specifically against SOPIPA's and Education Law 2-d's marketing-purpose bans; then build out the Google Business Profile, paid search, content and email layers that work the same way they do for any other local business, now that the data feeding them is clean. The education businesses that grow fastest here aren't the ones avoiding data-driven marketing — they're the ones that built consent and directory-notice checks into the workflow instead of finding the gap after a parent complaint or a state inquiry.

Frequently asked questions

Can a tutoring business or school post a student's name and photo in marketing materials?

If the business is itself a school or works inside one, usually only through FERPA's directory-information mechanism: the school must have given public notice of what counts as directory information (often name, photo, grade level) and a window for parents or eligible students to opt out under 34 CFR 99.37, and anyone who opted out cannot be included. A private tutoring company with no school affiliation is not itself bound by FERPA (which applies to schools and agencies receiving federal education funding, not private businesses), but it still needs the family's own consent before using a minor's name or image in an ad, and should treat any list of parent-opt-out requests it receives from a partner school as binding.

Can an online course platform run retargeting or behavioral ads to users under 13?

Not without specific, separate consent. COPPA (16 CFR Part 312) covers any operator of a website or online service directed to children under 13, or one with actual knowledge it is collecting personal information from a child under 13. The FTC's COPPA Rule amendments, published in the Federal Register on April 22, 2025 and effective June 23, 2025 (with a compliance deadline of April 22, 2026), added a requirement for separate, specific opt-in parental consent before using a child's personal information for targeted advertising — that consent can't be bundled into a general sign-up consent. A platform that can't show it has that consent should not be running retargeting pixels or ad-network tags on any page serving under-13 users.

Does FERPA apply to a private tutoring company or edtech startup that isn't itself a school?

Not directly in most cases. FERPA (20 U.S.C. § 1232g; 34 CFR Part 99) binds educational agencies and institutions that receive funding from the US Department of Education, not private companies on their own. But a private tutoring business, edtech platform or marketing vendor working with a school typically takes on FERPA-equivalent obligations through its contract with that school, and separately may fall under COPPA (if it collects data from under-13 users), the FTC's 2022 Ed Tech Policy Statement (if a school authorized its use of student data, that data can't be repurposed for the company's own advertising or marketing), and state laws like California's SOPIPA or New York's Education Law 2-d, which restrict using K-12 student data for marketing or targeted advertising regardless of federal funding status.
