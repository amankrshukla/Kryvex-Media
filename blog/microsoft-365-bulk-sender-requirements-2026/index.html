Microsoft's Bulk Sender Rules: What the 550 5.7.515 Bounce Actually Means

Google and Yahoo's 2024 bulk-sender crackdown got most of the attention. Microsoft has been quietly enforcing its own version against Outlook.com and Hotmail.com — and a 550 5.7.515 rejection is what you see when your authentication doesn't meet the bar. Here's what Microsoft's own documentation says, what's genuinely confirmed, and where the sources disagree.

K
Kryvex Media Team
Nov 1, 2026 · 9 min read

Microsoft's own support documentation confirms that a 550 5.7.515 non-delivery report means the sending domain failed to meet the authentication level Microsoft requires of high-volume senders. That one line, published on Microsoft's support pages, is the most authoritative single fact available on this topic — everything built around it (when enforcement started, exactly which mailboxes it covers, how it relates to Microsoft 365 business tenants) comes from secondary industry reporting that doesn't fully agree with itself. This post separates what Microsoft itself has confirmed from what deliverability vendors claim, so you know which parts to trust outright and which to verify against your own inbox placement data.

What a 550 5.7.515 bounce actually tells you

It's an authentication failure code, not a content or spam judgment. If you're seeing this bounce, it means the receiving Microsoft server checked the authentication posture of the domain in your message's From address — the combination of SPF, DKIM, and DMARC it has on file — and that domain didn't meet the bar Microsoft sets for senders above its volume threshold. The email itself may be perfectly legitimate, well-written, and wanted by the recipient; the rejection happens at the authentication layer before content is ever evaluated.

Don't confuse this with a 550 5.7.509 bounce, which is a separate DMARC-specific failure that can occur outside the bulk-sender rule entirely. If you're troubleshooting a bounce, check the exact code — the fix for each is related but not identical.

Who the threshold applies to

Per Microsoft's published guidance, the bulk-sender requirements are aimed at senders sending 5,000 or more messages per day to Microsoft's consumer email services — Outlook.com, Hotmail.com, and related consumer domains. If your sending volume to those specific domains sits under that threshold, you're not currently subject to the enforced rejection, though every sender benefits from solid SPF/DKIM/DMARC hygiene regardless of volume, since inbox providers' thresholds and enforcement have only moved in one direction over the past few years.

The genuinely unsettled question: does this cover Microsoft 365 business mail?

This is the point where the available sources actually disagree, and it's worth being upfront about that rather than flattening it into a confident claim. Some deliverability guides argue the 5.7.515 rule and its 5,000-message threshold target consumer mailboxes specifically, and that Microsoft 365 business tenants run a separate tenant-level filtering and policy system — overlapping in spirit (similar authentication checks) but not governed by the identical published rule. Other guides describe Microsoft 365 business mail as falling under its own, distinct rules that "apply similar checks in practice" without being the same mechanism. Microsoft's own support page — the single most authoritative source in this research — addresses the consumer-side 5.7.515 code specifically and doesn't resolve this business-tenant question explicitly in the material available.

The practical takeaway: if your sends go only to Microsoft 365 business addresses (a company's own @yourcompany.com hosted on Microsoft 365), don't assume the exact 5,000-message consumer threshold is the number that governs you — check your own bounce codes and reputation signals for that specific tenant rather than relying on the consumer-focused guidance. If you send meaningfully to Outlook.com or Hotmail.com addresses as part of a consumer-facing list, the 5.7.515 rule and its threshold is the one to plan against directly.

What the requirements actually are

Across the sources that cite Microsoft's own guidance consistently, the core requirements mirror what Google and Yahoo put in place in 2024:

  • SPF (Sender Policy Framework) — authorizes which mail servers can send on behalf of your domain.
  • DKIM (DomainKeys Identified Mail) — cryptographically signs outgoing mail so receiving servers can verify it wasn't altered or spoofed in transit.
  • DMARC (Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting and Conformance) — tells receiving servers what to do when a message fails SPF or DKIM, and reports back on authentication failures.
  • A working one-click unsubscribe and a genuinely reply-capable sender address are cited as best practice alongside the hard authentication requirements, consistent with what Google and Yahoo already require.

If you've already done the work to comply with Google and Yahoo's 2024 bulk-sender requirements — covered in our Gmail and Yahoo sender requirements guide — you've already built most of what Microsoft is checking for. SPF, DKIM, and DMARC set up correctly satisfy the authentication layer across all three providers; the gap, if there is one, is usually in not having checked whether your DMARC policy is actually at enforcement (`p=quarantine` or `p=reject`) rather than sitting at the permissive `p=none` monitoring-only setting many senders never move past.

Enforcement timeline, per the available reporting

Industry trackers describe enforcement as having started in May 2025, beginning with junk-folder routing for non-compliant senders before escalating to outright rejection (the 5.7.515 bounce) by around September 2025. We found no confirmed 2026-specific change to the underlying rule itself in this research — the rule appears to be the same one that's been in force since mid-to-late 2025, with 2026 content mostly being vendors restating and re-promoting the existing requirement rather than describing something new. If you're reading "2026 changes" framing elsewhere, treat it skeptically unless it cites a specific, dated Microsoft announcement.

How to check your own setup

  1. Confirm SPF, DKIM, and DMARC are all published correctly for every domain you send marketing or transactional email from — not just your primary domain if you use subdomains for different send types.
  2. Check your DMARC policy's enforcement level. `p=none` only monitors; it doesn't protect you from the bulk-sender checks the way `p=quarantine` or `p=reject` does.
  3. Add a genuine one-click unsubscribe (via the `List-Unsubscribe` header, not just a link buried in the footer) if you haven't already — most major email service providers (Mailchimp, Klaviyo, and similar) support this natively.
  4. If you're specifically sending to a consumer-facing list with Outlook.com or Hotmail.com addresses at meaningful volume, monitor your bounce logs for 5.7.515 codes directly rather than assuming compliance.
  5. If you send primarily to business Microsoft 365 addresses, don't rely on the consumer threshold number — watch your own sender reputation signals and bounce codes for that traffic specifically.

Why this matters even if you're a small sender

Most small businesses don't send anywhere close to 5,000 messages a day to Microsoft's consumer domains, so the hard 5.7.515 rejection may never directly hit you. But the broader direction — major mailbox providers steadily raising the authentication bar and enforcing it with outright rejections rather than soft spam-folder routing — has been consistent across Google, Yahoo, and now Microsoft for two straight years. A business building its email list and automation flows today, covered in our guide to the email flows every business needs, is better served treating full SPF/DKIM/DMARC setup as a day-one requirement rather than something to retrofit after a growth spurt pushes volume past a threshold you weren't tracking.

There's also a reputation effect that doesn't require hitting any specific volume threshold to matter. Inbox providers, including Microsoft, factor broader sender reputation signals — complaint rates, engagement, how consistently a domain's authentication checks out over time — into where mail lands even below the hard enforcement threshold. A domain with spotty or missing DKIM signing can see mail routed to spam or clipped in preview even without ever triggering an outright 5.7.515 rejection, because reputation scoring isn't a strict on/off switch tied only to the published volume number. In practice, that means a growing business is better off building correct authentication in from the first campaign rather than waiting until send volume crosses 5,000 a day to take it seriously — by the time volume gets there, a domain with a thin reputation history is already at a disadvantage relative to one that's been authenticating cleanly from day one.

What this looks like if you're switching email platforms

This is also a relevant moment to flag for any business planning to switch email service providers — a common trigger for broken authentication that has nothing to do with Microsoft's rules specifically. When a business moves from one ESP to another (say, from a basic newsletter tool to a full marketing automation platform), SPF records in particular are easy to misconfigure: a new platform needs to be added to the domain's SPF record as an authorized sender, and simply forgetting that step, or leaving a stale SPF entry for a long-abandoned platform, is a common, avoidable source of authentication failures. DKIM keys are specific to the sending platform too, so a migration generally means generating and publishing new DKIM records for the new provider, not reusing the old ones. Anyone handling a platform migration should treat re-verifying SPF, DKIM, and DMARC as a required step in the cutover checklist, not an assumption that "it worked before, so it'll keep working."

Frequently asked questions

What does a 550 5.7.515 bounce from Outlook or Microsoft mean?

According to Microsoft's own support documentation, a 550 5.7.515 non-delivery report means the domain in the message's From address failed to meet the authentication level Microsoft requires of high-volume senders. It is an authentication failure, not a spam-content judgment on the email itself.

What is Microsoft's threshold for being treated as a bulk sender?

Per Microsoft's published guidance, the bulk-sender requirements apply to senders sending 5,000 or more messages per day to Microsoft's consumer email services (Outlook.com, Hotmail.com, and similar). Smaller-volume senders are not currently held to the same enforced threshold, though good authentication practice benefits every sender regardless of volume.

Do Microsoft 365 business email requirements work the same way as the consumer bulk-sender rules?

Not necessarily, and sources disagree on exactly how much overlap there is. The 5.7.515 bulk-sender enforcement documented by Microsoft is specifically aimed at consumer mailboxes like Outlook.com and Hotmail.com. Microsoft 365 business tenants run their own, separate filtering and policy layer, which applies broadly similar authentication logic in practice but isn't governed by the identical published threshold. If you send to both consumer and business Microsoft addresses, don't assume one fix covers both.
