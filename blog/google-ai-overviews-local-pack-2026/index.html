Google's AI Overviews Are Starting to Replace the Local 3-Pack — What It Means in 2026

The local 3-pack — the familiar map plus three business listings that's shown up for "near me" searches for over a decade — isn't universal anymore. Local SEO consultant Joy Hawkins documented, and Search Engine Roundtable reported in January 2026, that Google is increasingly answering local queries with an AI-generated overview instead, sometimes surfacing just one or two businesses rather than three. Here's what's actually been documented, what's still unconfirmed agency speculation, and what it changes about doing local SEO well.

K
Kryvex Media Team
Nov 4, 2026 · 10 min read

For well over a decade, "search for it and see three businesses on a map" has been the default shape of local search. That default is starting to crack in a documented, trackable way. In January 2026, Search Engine Roundtable — one of the longest-running outlets covering real-time Google search behavior — reported on findings from Joy Hawkins, a local SEO consultant at Sterling Sky who has tracked local search results closely for years. Her observation, corroborated independently by fellow search commentator Glenn Gabe in a January 15, 2026 post, is straightforward and concerning for local business owners: Google is increasingly answering local-intent queries with an AI-generated overview instead of the traditional map pack, and some businesses are seeing visibility declines of 50% or more as a direct result.

What's actually been documented

The core, well-sourced finding is this: Hawkins has published numerous side-by-side examples showing the same local query returning a classic 3-pack in one instance and an AI-generated local answer in another — and the AI-generated version frequently surfaces only one or two businesses, not three. That's a meaningfully smaller set of visible positions for the identical search intent. Hawkins has also documented cases where the businesses named in the AI-generated answer don't match the businesses shown in the map pack for the same query — a mismatch that makes the AI version harder to predict or optimize for using the ranking signals local SEOs have relied on for years.

On scale, Hawkins' tracking cites data from a tool called Places Scout showing AI Local Packs already appearing on roughly 8% of tracked local keywords, with that share described as actively rising rather than holding steady. This isn't a universal replacement of the local pack — the large majority of local searches were still returning the classic format as of the reporting reviewed here — but an 8%-and-growing share is large enough that most local businesses with any meaningful search visibility have likely already been affected by it for at least some of their target queries, even if they haven't noticed the specific cause.

Context matters here too: this isn't Google's first attempt at blending AI answers with local results. A separate, earlier Search Engine Roundtable report noted that Google had removed the local pack from AI Overviews back in September 2023, then brought it back in March 2025. The January 2026 reporting describes a further evolution of that same ongoing experiment — not a brand-new feature appearing out of nowhere, but a continuation of a multi-year pattern of Google testing how local results and AI-generated answers coexist.

What's real data versus agency estimate

It's worth being precise about which numbers in this story are solidly sourced and which are agency-blog estimates that shouldn't be treated the same way. The Hawkins/Search Engine Roundtable reporting — the 50%+ visibility-decline examples, the 8% keyword-coverage figure from Places Scout, and the documented mismatches between AI answers and the map pack — is the most directly evidenced material here, coming from someone who tracks this specific phenomenon professionally and whose findings were independently corroborated by a second well-known search commentator.

Beyond that core finding, several marketing-agency blog posts covering the same trend cite additional statistics that conflict with each other and don't trace to a disclosed, verifiable methodology: one agency analysis claims AI-generated local packs cover only about 32% of the businesses a legacy 3-pack would show; a separate source puts Google Business Profile's share of local-pack ranking influence at 32% while yet another puts the comparable AI-search-visibility figure at 12%; another claims local-intent queries trigger AI Overviews only about 15% of the time versus 93% for the classic pack, with the pattern reversed for informational queries. None of these specific percentages are confirmed by Google or independently verified in the reporting examined here, and several directly conflict with each other — so they're presented in this piece only as context for how actively SEO agencies are trying to quantify a trend that Google itself hasn't published numbers on, not as established facts to build a strategy around.

Treat any specific percentage you see quoted for "how often AI replaces the local pack" or "how much GBP data drives AI local answers" with real skepticism unless it traces to Google's own documentation or a named, methodologically transparent tracking study. The agency-blog figures circulating on this topic conflict with each other by wide margins, which is itself a sign none of them should be treated as settled.

Why this isn't actually a reason to panic — or to do anything radically different

Here's the part that should be genuinely reassuring rather than alarming: nothing in the documented reporting suggests Google is using a fundamentally different set of signals to decide which businesses deserve local visibility. Hawkins' own commentary, and the broader pattern Google has followed with AI Overviews generally, points to the AI-generated answer drawing from the same underlying data — Google Business Profile completeness and accuracy, review volume and recency, proximity, and website content that clearly answers what a searcher is actually asking — that has always driven local pack rankings. The format of the answer is changing in a real, documented way; the fundamental inputs that earn visibility in either format are not.

That means the actionable response isn't a reinvention of local SEO strategy, it's a renewed emphasis on doing the fundamentals thoroughly rather than adequately. A Google Business Profile with complete categories, accurate hours, real photos, and an active flow of genuine reviews remains the foundation either way. Website content that answers specific, narrow local questions directly — "do you offer same-day appointments," "what's parking like near your location," "do you serve [specific neighborhood]" — gives an AI-generated answer system more concrete, extractable material to work with than a generic services page does, the same way it's long helped with featured snippets and voice search. If anything, the shift toward fewer visible slots in AI-generated answers raises the stakes for getting those fundamentals right, because there's less room for a middling profile to still show up by default alongside two stronger competitors.

What to actually check this month

Rather than chasing unverified percentages, the practical move is to check your own visibility directly. Search your own top 10-15 "near me" and city-plus-service queries from a logged-out browser or incognito window and note which format each one returns — classic 3-pack, AI-generated answer, or something else — and whether your business appears in it. That gives you a real, business-specific baseline instead of a generic industry statistic. If you find queries where an AI-generated answer is already showing and you're not in it, that's a concrete signal to audit your Google Business Profile completeness and recent review activity for that specific query's intent, rather than a reason to overhaul your entire local SEO approach based on an unverified industry-wide number.

One more thing worth noting for context: this pattern of AI-generated answers reshuffling who's visible isn't unique to local search. Kryvex has covered a related dynamic in a separate look at how Google's AI Mode cites sources differently from standard AI Overviews for local queries specifically — the two features don't pull from identical sources or weight them the same way, which is one more reason a single AI-generated answer shouldn't be treated as the definitive signal for how visible a business actually is across Google's various local-search surfaces. A business that disappears from one AI-generated answer format may still appear normally in the classic pack, in AI Mode, or in a different AI Overview variant for a slightly different phrasing of the same underlying question — which is exactly why checking your own queries directly, across a reasonable sample size, beats reacting to any single screenshot or any single unverified industry statistic.

Frequently asked questions

Is Google replacing the local 3-pack with AI Overviews?

Not universally, but it's happening on a meaningful and growing share of searches. Search Engine Roundtable reported in January 2026 on findings from local SEO consultant Joy Hawkins (Sterling Sky) showing some businesses seeing visibility declines of 50% or more where an AI-generated local answer displaces the traditional map pack, and citing Places Scout tracking data showing AI Local Packs appearing on roughly 8% of keywords, a share described as rising.

How many businesses show up in an AI-generated local pack versus the classic one?

Documented examples show AI-generated local answers often surfacing just one or two businesses, compared to the three businesses shown in Google's traditional local pack. That's a meaningfully smaller set of visible slots for the same local search intent.

What should a local business actually do about this change?

Keep investing in the fundamentals that feed both the classic local pack and AI-generated answers: a complete, accurate Google Business Profile, a steady flow of genuine customer reviews, and website content that directly and specifically answers the questions real customers ask. These are the same signals Google has always used for local relevance, and they remain the most defensible way to stay visible regardless of which local search format a given query triggers.
