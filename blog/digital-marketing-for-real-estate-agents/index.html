Digital Marketing for Real Estate Agents: What Actually Generates Listings

Real estate runs on a strange split: most business still closes through referral and repeat relationships, yet the search for a home — and increasingly the search for the agent — starts online, usually on a phone. An agent's marketing has to win that first online moment and then feed the referral engine that actually closes deals. Here's how the pieces genuinely fit together.

K
Kryvex Media Team
Oct 22, 2026 · 10 min read

Real estate marketing has a contradiction built into it that most other industries don't deal with in quite the same way. The decision of which agent to trust with the largest financial transaction most people will ever make is still, overwhelmingly, driven by referral and past relationships. And yet the search that leads to that decision — browsing listings, checking a neighborhood, looking up an agent's name — starts online, on a phone, often at night or on a weekend. Marketing that only optimizes for the online moment misses why people actually choose an agent. Marketing that only relies on referral misses the growing share of the funnel that never touches a referral at all until an online search happens first. Below is how the two actually connect, and what to build in which order.

Referrals still close deals — but the first touch increasingly happens online

Secondary summaries of NAR's 2025 Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers (covering transactions closed between July 2024 and June 2025) report that 88% of buyers purchased through an agent or broker and 91% of sellers used an agent — both figures described as record or near-record highs for the survey. Underneath that, the same research shows referral and past relationships doing most of the work of choosing which agent: 43% of buyers were referred by a friend, neighbor or relative (49% for first-time buyers, 41% for repeat buyers), and 66% of sellers either used a referral or an agent they'd worked with before, split roughly between a direct referral (38%) and a past working relationship (28%).

None of that means online marketing doesn't matter — it means it's doing a different job than closing the relationship. NAR's own listing for the same 2025 Profile states that 52% of buyers found the actual home they purchased online, and 70% used a mobile or tablet device at some point during their search. Put together, the realistic picture is a funnel where online channels do most of the work of surfacing properties and agents to people who have no existing relationship yet, and referral and past-client trust do most of the work of converting that initial contact into a signed client — which is exactly why an agent's marketing has to cover both halves rather than picking one.

If referral and repeat business are already the dominant source of closings per NAR's own research, the marketing question isn't "how do I get more referrals" in the abstract — it's "what system actually keeps past clients close enough to refer," which is a nurture and follow-up problem, not a discovery problem.

Google's local pack runs on the same three rules for every business — including yours

Google's own Business Profile help documentation describes local search results as built on three factors: relevance (how well a profile matches what someone searched for), distance (how far the result is from the location implied in the search), and prominence (how well-known the business is, drawn from signals across the web including reviews, articles and directory mentions). Google states this explicitly and unambiguously: there is no way to pay for a better local ranking. That makes a complete, accurately categorized Google Business Profile — correct service area, real office address if there is one, categories that actually match "real estate agent" rather than a generic business category, and a steady stream of genuine post-closing reviews — one of the only local-visibility levers that's entirely free and entirely within an agent's control, which is exactly why it's worth getting right before spending on anything paid. Our local SEO team and our Google Business Profile optimization guide cover the same relevance/distance/prominence mechanics in more depth, since they apply identically whether the business is a real estate office or a restaurant.

What IDX actually does on an agent's own website

A real estate website without a working IDX feed is a brochure: bio, headshot, a contact form, and no actual inventory. IDX (Internet Data Exchange) is the licensed pipeline that changes that. MLS organizations — the regional databases that store listings submitted by participating brokerages — license that data to approved IDX vendors, and those vendors distribute the feed to individual agent and brokerage websites, where it stays synced on a regular update cycle rather than going stale. The mechanism underneath IDX is a reciprocity rule: a broker who opts in agrees to let other participating brokers display their listings, in exchange for the right to display everyone else's listings too. A smaller number of markets also offer a VOW (Virtual Office Website) option, which can carry more listing data than IDX but typically gates it behind a visitor registration wall — most boards have moved toward the less-restrictive IDX model specifically because consumers tend to abandon a site that demands registration before they can browse. Without IDX actually connected and working, a prospect who lands on an agent's own site and can't search live listings there has every reason to bounce straight back to a portal instead — which is the opposite of what the website was built to do. This is foundational enough to the funnel that our web design team treats a working IDX connection as a non-negotiable requirement for any real estate site build, not an optional add-on.

What Zillow and realtor.com actually sell you — and what's genuinely free

A basic agent profile claim on the major portals is largely a free entry point, and it's worth doing regardless of anything else, since it's often where an online search for an agent's name actually lands. The paid layer on top is a different product. Per Zillow's own description of Zillow Premier Agent, agents choose the ZIP codes they want leads in and buy a share of the total ad spend running in that area; that share determines how large a portion of the leads generated there gets routed to them. Those leads fall into a few distinct categories — a direct contact (a consumer who picked that specific agent from the start), a connection (a buyer who clicked "contact agent" generically and was then matched and verified before being routed), and a nurture lead (someone earlier in the process, not yet ready to talk to an agent) — managed through an app with its own inbox and built-in CRM. The practical read for an agent deciding whether to spend here: this is a paid-advertising channel competing for visibility inside someone else's platform, priced by local demand and competition in that ZIP code, not a search-visibility or SEO play. It tends to work best layered on top of an owned, IDX-connected website and a complete Google Business Profile — supplementing the channels an agent actually controls, rather than substituting for them.

Content and visuals: the baseline buyers expect has moved

Listing photography and walkthrough video have gone from a differentiator to a baseline expectation on both the agent's own site and every portal a listing syndicates to — buyers scanning a results page or a local pack decide which listing is worth a click largely on the strength of the first image. Google Business Profile itself supports photo and short-video uploads directly on the listing, which is additional free real estate worth using rather than leaving the default placeholder in place. Beyond individual listings, genuinely useful content — neighborhood guides, honest buyer and seller process explainers, local market write-ups — gives an agent something to rank for before a prospect has even picked a property, which is exactly the kind of early-funnel visibility a local SEO content strategy is built around, and it's the same discipline our content marketing team runs for real estate clients specifically because the content needs differ from a transactional retail or restaurant page.

Email and CRM: the system that turns one closing into the referral NAR's own data says is normal

Given that referral and past-client relationships already account for a large share of real NAR-measured closings, the highest-leverage marketing system for most agents isn't another acquisition channel — it's the follow-up system that keeps a past client close enough to actually refer, or to come back when they move again. A simple structure covers most of it: a closing-day and first-anniversary touchpoint, a periodic local market update that's genuinely useful rather than a thinly disguised ad, and a check-in that makes referring easy by being specific about it rather than a vague "let me know if you hear of anyone." This is the same category of automated sequence our broader email marketing work builds for other industries, but for real estate specifically it's acting directly on the referral mechanism the data above already shows is doing most of the real work — not creating a new channel from scratch.

The mistakes that quietly cap a real estate marketing budget

  • Treating Zillow or realtor.com spend as a substitute for an owned website. Paid portal leads are rented visibility inside someone else's platform; an IDX-connected site is the only channel that keeps compounding in value under the agent's own control.
  • An incomplete or miscategorized Google Business Profile. One of the few local-visibility levers that's completely free, and one of the most commonly left half-finished.
  • No real past-client nurture system. Given how much of real closings trace back to referral or repeat business per NAR's own research, skipping this is skipping the highest-leverage channel available.
  • A website with no working IDX feed. It turns an owned asset into a brochure with no actual reason for a visitor to stay and search.
  • Listing photos and visuals that don't meet the current baseline. On a page where buyers decide whether to click almost entirely on the first image, this is an easy, visible gap.

Where to actually start

In rough priority order: get the Google Business Profile complete and accurately categorized first, since it's free and directly shapes local-pack visibility; make sure the website has a genuinely working IDX feed second, so there's an owned destination for that visibility to land on; build the past-client email and CRM nurture system third, since it's acting directly on the referral and repeat-business mechanism that NAR's own data shows already drives most closings; add neighborhood and buyer/seller-process content fourth, to capture earlier-funnel searches before a property is picked; and treat paid portal leads like Zillow Premier Agent as a fifth, supplementary layer once the first four owned channels are solid — not a replacement for any of them. Most agents do this in the opposite order, spending on portal leads first because it's the fastest thing to turn on, which is exactly why leads generated that way often convert at a lower rate than they could.

Frequently asked questions

How much of real estate business actually comes from referrals versus online search?

Both matter, but at different stages. Secondary summaries of NAR's 2025 Profile of Home Buyers and Sellers report that 43% of buyers found their agent through a referral from a friend, neighbor or relative, and that 66% of sellers used either a referral or an agent they'd worked with before. At the same time, NAR's own listing for that report states 52% of buyers found the actual home they purchased online, and 70% used a mobile or tablet device at some point during the search. Online channels tend to drive discovery of the agent and the property; referral and past relationships tend to drive the final decision of who to work with.

Can a real estate agent pay Google for a better local search ranking?

No. Google's own Business Profile help documentation states explicitly that there is no way to pay for a better local ranking on Google. Local results are based on relevance, distance and prominence — the same three factors Google applies to every business category, with no paid override.

What's the actual difference between IDX and just having a website?

A website without a working IDX feed is a brochure — it describes the agent but shows no live inventory. IDX is the licensed pipeline that lets an agent's own site display real, regularly updated MLS listings: the MLS licenses its data to approved IDX vendors, who distribute that feed to agent and brokerage websites under a broker-reciprocity rule, where any broker opting in agrees to display other participants' listings in exchange for having their own listings displayed elsewhere.

Is Zillow Premier Agent worth it for a new agent?

It can generate real leads, but it's a paid-advertising channel, not a substitute for an owned website or Google Business Profile. Per Zillow's own description of the product, agents buy a share of ad spend in chosen ZIP codes, and that share determines how many leads — split into direct contacts, connections and nurture leads — get routed to them each month in that area. It tends to work best layered on top of an IDX-connected website and a complete Google Business Profile, not in place of either.
