Gmail's AI Summaries Are Changing How People Read Marketing Emails

On January 8, 2026, Google rolled Gemini-powered features into Gmail — most notably, an AI-generated one or two sentence summary that appears automatically when a user opens a longer email. For a channel that's already spent years fighting unreliable open-rate data, this is a real, dated change worth understanding properly rather than reacting to with a vague sense that "AI is coming for email too." Here's what's actually been announced, what the early industry data suggests, and what's worth changing about how you write marketing emails.

K
Kryvex Media Team
Nov 4, 2026 · 9 min read

Email marketing's measurement problems didn't start on January 8, 2026 — but that's the date a new one got added to the pile. Google announced Gemini-powered features for Gmail on that Thursday, describing the update as turning the inbox into more of a proactive assistant for its roughly 3 billion users worldwide. The headline consumer-facing feature, often referred to in coverage as Gmail's "AI Overviews," is an automatically generated summary — typically one to two sentences — that appears when a user opens a longer email, pulling out what the reporting describes as the key message, decisions, and next steps. For anyone sending marketing or newsletter email, that's a genuinely new variable sitting between a subject line and whatever's inside the email itself, and it's worth understanding on its own terms rather than through vague "AI is disrupting email" framing.

What Google actually announced, and what's still limited

The rollout itself has several tiers worth knowing about, because not every feature is available the same way. The automatic thread and email summaries are rolling out to all Gmail users at no cost — this is the piece that directly affects how marketing emails get read. A more advanced capability, letting users ask their inbox natural-language questions, is restricted to subscribers of Google's paid AI Pro and Ultra tiers. A separate "AI Inbox" experience, which goes further toward an assistant-style interface, was described at launch as still in limited testing with a small group of trusted users rather than broadly available. Writing-assistance tools — an updated "Help Me Write" and smarter Suggested Replies — are free for everyone, while a more advanced Proofread feature stays behind the subscription paywall. The rollout itself began in the US for English-language users on the announcement date, with Google stating plans to expand to more regions and languages over the following months — worth noting if a meaningful share of your list is outside the US or non-English-language, since the summary behavior may not have reached those recipients yet.

Google's own framing around privacy is worth including rather than skipping: the company said Gemini processes inbox data to generate these summaries and answers, with that processing kept behind what Google describes as a secure "engineering privacy" barrier. That's a company statement, not an independently audited claim, but it's the specific language Google used and is worth citing accurately rather than paraphrasing into something stronger or weaker than what was actually said.

What the early data suggests — and why some of it should be read skeptically

Because this feature is still young, most of what's circulating about its real-world effect on email metrics comes from individual industry analyses rather than a comprehensive, independently verified study, and that distinction matters for how much weight to put on any single number. One such analysis, cited secondhand through an email-marketing vendor's blog, reported that click-through rate fell from roughly 4.35% to about 3.93% after Gmail's AI summaries went live for the accounts it tracked, while the same accounts' reported open rate actually rose to around 45.6%. On its face, a rising open rate paired with a falling click rate sounds contradictory — but the likely explanation, which several sources in this space raise independently, is mechanical rather than behavioral: Gmail has to open an email in order to generate its summary, and some email service providers' tracking pixels register that automatic, AI-driven open the same way they'd register a human opening the email. If that's happening at any real scale, "open rate" becomes an even less trustworthy number than it already was.

That unreliability isn't new, which is useful context rather than an excuse to ignore the new wrinkle. Apple's Mail Privacy Protection, introduced in 2021, has pre-fetched and "opened" emails for privacy reasons for several years now, already inflating open-rate data for any subscriber using Apple Mail — a problem the email marketing industry has broadly adapted to by leaning more heavily on click-through and conversion data instead of open rate as the primary performance signal. Gmail's AI summaries plausibly extend a version of that same inflation problem to Google's user base, which is considerably larger than Apple Mail's. One vendor-sourced figure claiming a specific drop in Gmail's overall inbox placement rate circulated in coverage of this topic, but it could not be traced to a primary source or independently verified, so it's deliberately left out of this piece rather than repeated as fact — a instructive example of exactly the kind of plausible-sounding number that shouldn't make it into a client-facing report without a real source behind it.

The 4.35%-to-3.93% click-through figure and the 45.6% open-rate figure above trace to a single industry analysis relayed through a secondary blog, not a disclosed, independently audited study — treat them as one data point worth watching, not an industry-wide benchmark to build a strategy around.

What this actually changes about writing a marketing email

The practical takeaway isn't to panic about AI "reading" your emails for subscribers — it's to recognize that Gmail's AI summary functions as something close to a second subject line, one you don't get to write directly but can meaningfully influence. Because the summary is generated from the email's actual content rather than its subject line or preview text, the opening sentence or two of the email body now carries more real weight than it used to for Gmail recipients specifically. An email that opens with a clear, concrete statement of what it's actually about — the specific offer, the specific update, the specific next step — gives Gemini's summarization model cleaner material to work with than an email that opens with a generic greeting or a stock "Hope you're doing well" line before getting to the point several sentences in. That's not a wild AI-era hack; it's the same "front-load the point" advice that's always made emails easier for a human skimming an inbox to act on, now mattering for an additional, automated reason.

On measurement, the sensible adjustment is one most email marketers should have made already because of Apple's Mail Privacy Protection, and Gmail's rollout is a second, independent reason to actually make it if you haven't: treat open rate as a soft, directional signal at best, and make click-through rate, reply rate, and downstream conversion the metrics that actually drive decisions about subject lines, send times, and list segmentation. If your reporting dashboard still leads with open rate as the headline metric, this is a reasonable moment to restructure it — not because open rate just became meaningless, but because it's now unreliable from two separate, well-documented directions rather than one.

There's a reasonable comparison to draw with how search marketers have already adapted to AI Overviews appearing above organic results in Google Search: the response wasn't to stop writing good content, it was to write content that answers the specific question clearly enough to be extractable, whether a human or a summarization model is doing the reading. The same logic applies here. A marketing email that's been padded with throat-clearing before the actual point — a long preamble about the season, the company's values, or a generic greeting — was already weaker copy before January 2026; it's simply more visibly weaker now that an automated summary is extracting from the first few sentences specifically. None of this requires new software or a different email service provider. It requires the same discipline good email copywriting has always rewarded: say the actual thing early, make the subject line and the opening line do real work together, and measure results with metrics that reflect whether someone acted on the email rather than whether an inbox technically registered it as opened.

Frequently asked questions

When did Gmail roll out AI-generated email summaries?

Google announced Gemini-powered features for Gmail on January 8, 2026, including automatic AI-generated summaries that display when a user opens a longer email. The rollout began that Thursday for English-language users in the US, with Google saying it planned to expand to more regions and languages over the following months.

Does Gmail's AI summary feature inflate open rates?

It plausibly can, because Gmail has to open an email to generate its summary, and some email service providers count that automatic action as a human open. This is a similar dynamic to the one Apple's Mail Privacy Protection created starting in 2021, which already made open-rate data for Apple Mail users unreliable; Gmail's AI summaries raise the same concern for Gmail's much larger user base.

What should a business actually change about its email marketing because of this?

Shift primary attention toward click-through rate, reply rate, and conversion data rather than open rate, since open rate was already compromised by Apple's Mail Privacy Protection and is now further complicated by Gmail's auto-generated summaries. It's also worth testing how your own emails' opening lines render inside Gmail's AI summary, since that one or two sentence summary functions as a second subject line that determines whether a recipient bothers to open the full email at all.
