Digital Marketing for Tour Operators and Travel Agencies: What a $35 Million FTC Case Just Changed

On July 2, 2026, the FTC announced that travel app Hopper agreed to pay $35 million to settle allegations that it advertised "no hidden fees" while charging consumers undisclosed "Tip" and VIP Support fees — one of the first enforcement actions under the agency's Rule on Unfair or Deceptive Fees, which took effect in May 2025 and reaches far beyond one app. That rule, a separate federal ban on fake and bought reviews, and the FTC's revised rules on influencer disclosure now sit directly underneath how every tour operator, travel agency and destination marketer can legally advertise a price, display a review, or run a famtrip influencer campaign. None of this means travel marketing has to get more cautious across the board — it means knowing exactly where the real lines are before a campaign, a pricing page or an influencer partnership crosses one.

Travel marketing runs on three things that other local businesses handle differently: a price that's rarely final until checkout, a review score that drives the actual booking decision more than almost any other industry, and a roster of influencer and famtrip partnerships most other sectors don't use at this scale. Each of those now sits underneath a real, current federal rule — a total-price disclosure requirement for lodging and ticketed travel, a ban on fake or bought reviews, and a disclosure rule for any paid or comped influencer trip — and a $35 million settlement announced this year shows the FTC is actively enforcing the first of those against a travel company specifically, not a hypothetical. The underlying growth channels — local search for destination and "near me" travel queries, paid search against high-intent booking terms, content that answers real trip-planning questions, and a review-generation program — work the same way they do for any other business. What's different is the compliance layer sitting directly on top of the channels travel marketing depends on most.

The $35 million case that just redrew the line on travel pricing

On July 2, 2026, the FTC announced that Hopper Inc. and Hopper (USA) Inc. — the companies behind the Hopper travel-booking app — agreed to a $35 million settlement over allegations that directly concern how travel pricing gets advertised online. The FTC's complaint alleges that despite marketing itself around a "no hidden fees" promise, Hopper charged consumers "Tip" and VIP Support fees that were pre-selected by default and not clearly disclosed — in one instance, according to the FTC, a fee was visible only if a user scrolled down past the point where most people stop looking. The agency also alleged Hopper overstated what its paid VIP Support and Price Freeze add-ons actually delivered, with many paying customers reportedly unable to reach a support agent or facing long delays once they'd paid for faster service. One internal company message cited directly in the FTC's materials reads: "To me, the problem here is that we're tricking users." Under the settlement, Hopper must pay $35 million in consumer redress, is barred from misrepresenting fees going forward, and must clearly and conspicuously disclose all charges and the total price before a consumer pays — and commentary on the case notes the FTC is relying in part on its own Rule on Unfair or Deceptive Fees, which had only been in effect a little over a year at the time of the complaint. Whatever happens next in that specific matter, the case itself is a real, current statement of what the FTC considers deceptive travel-fee practice — not a theoretical risk sitting in a regulation nobody has tested yet.

The Junk Fees Rule: what it actually requires when you advertise a price

The rule behind that enforcement theory has a name most travel marketers should already know: the FTC's Rule on Unfair or Deceptive Fees, codified at 16 CFR Part 464 and commonly called the "Junk Fees Rule." It was finalized in December 2024, published in the Federal Register on January 10, 2025, and took effect on May 12, 2025. Its scope is narrower than the FTC's original 2023 proposal, but it lands squarely on travel: the final rule covers any business that offers short-term lodging — hotels, vacation rentals, and other short-term stays — or live-event tickets, and its text specifically reaches third-party platforms, resellers, and travel agents, not only the hotel or venue selling directly. The rule's two core requirements are straightforward to state and easy to get wrong in practice: the business must display the total price, including all mandatory fees, clearly and prominently before asking the consumer to pay — a "starting from" rate that balloons at checkout with a resort fee, service fee, or booking fee that wasn't shown earlier doesn't satisfy this — and it is separately illegal to misrepresent the nature, purpose, or amount of any fee or charge in an ad or offer, even where a total price is eventually shown. Civil penalties for a knowing violation can run as high as roughly $51,000 per violation, a figure that is adjusted for inflation and tracked across FTC enforcement generally.

For a tour operator bundling a package price, a destination marketer advertising hotel rates it doesn't directly control, or a travel agency promoting third-party lodging, the practical takeaway is the same regardless of who technically sets the fee: a landing page, a paid-search ad extension, or a retargeting ad that shows a rate without the mandatory fees baked in is now a real compliance exposure, not just an aggressive pricing tactic.

Walk your own booking funnel the way a real customer would, screen by screen, on both desktop and mobile. The Hopper complaint turns on exactly this gap — a fee that existed on a screen most users wouldn't scroll to see. If the price shown in your ad, your landing page, or your first checkout step doesn't match the number a customer actually pays, that gap is the real exposure, independent of how the fee is technically labeled internally.

The Fake Reviews Rule: what travel marketing can no longer do with reviews

Few industries depend on review scores the way travel does — a hotel, tour operator, or destination's star rating and review volume often decide the booking before price even gets compared. That makes the FTC's Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials directly relevant to travel marketing specifically. The rule was finalized and published in the Federal Register on August 22, 2024, and took effect 60 days later, on October 21, 2024. It bans several practices that have historically been common in review-heavy industries: writing or buying reviews from people who don't exist or never actually used the service, including AI-generated reviews presented as real customer feedback; a business creating fake reviews of its own competitors; offering or soliciting a review in exchange for compensation or a free stay without disclosing that connection; an employee, owner, or affiliated party posting a review of their own business without disclosing the relationship; and suppressing or selectively hiding genuine negative reviews so only positive ones display publicly. Civil penalties for a knowing violation can exceed $50,000 per instance, and the rule gives the FTC a direct enforcement path that didn't exist in this form before late 2024.

None of this restricts asking satisfied guests or travelers for an honest review, or responding publicly to a negative one — both remain not just legal but genuinely effective marketing. What it closes off is the shortcut version: a purchased review package, a "leave us 5 stars and get $20 off your next trip" incentive that doesn't disclose the exchange, or a dashboard that only ever pushes positive reviews live while quietly withholding the rest.

Famtrips, influencer stays and the Endorsement Guides

Travel marketing leans on influencer and press trips — comped stays, free excursions, discounted tours — at a scale most other industries don't match, and that activity runs directly under the FTC's Guides Concerning the Use of Endorsements and Testimonials in Advertising (16 CFR Part 255), revised for the first time since 2009 on June 29, 2023. The governing rule is §255.5's material-connection requirement: whenever a typical viewer wouldn't otherwise assume a connection exists between the business and the person posting — a free flight, a comped hotel stay, a discounted tour, anything of value, even without an explicit requirement to post afterward — that connection has to be disclosed clearly and conspicuously in the post itself, not buried in a bio link or relied on through a platform's own default disclosure toggle, which the FTC's own guidance notes may not be prominent enough standing alone. The FTC's materials are also explicit that liability isn't limited to the business arranging the trip — the influencer and any agency or intermediary coordinating the campaign can be held responsible for an undisclosed endorsement as well. For a destination, tour operator, or hotel group running an active creator program, that means every famtrip itinerary, every comped stay, and every "gifted" product review needs a disclosure standard built into the campaign brief up front, not left to each creator's personal judgment.

Where the marketing mechanics are the same as any other business

Once pricing pages, review programs, and influencer disclosures are built to the standards above, a tour operator or travel agency grows through the same channels every other consumer business relies on. Local SEO and a complete, accurately categorized Google Business Profile remain the free, high-leverage foundation for any location-based tour, excursion, or travel-agency storefront — the same base our team builds for every local client. Paid search continues to perform strongly against high-intent booking queries ("tours in [destination]," "[activity] package," specific departure dates), provided the landing page's advertised price matches what a traveler is actually charged at checkout. Social media remains one of the highest-leverage channels in travel specifically, since destination and experience content is inherently visual — as long as every paid or comped post carries the disclosure the Endorsement Guides require. Content marketing — honest destination guides, real itinerary breakdowns, transparent answers about what's included in a package price — is a comparatively low-risk channel precisely because it doesn't depend on a single dramatic claim or an unverifiable review to work, and it directly supports the kind of real trip-planning search intent that drives bookings. And email marketing to an already-booked or opted-in traveler list sits largely outside the rules above, since it isn't a public price advertisement, a public review, or a public endorsement.

Mistakes that create real exposure, not just missed opportunity

  • Advertising a package, room, or ticket rate that excludes a mandatory fee shown only at checkout — this is the core fact pattern behind the FTC's $35 million case against Hopper and the exact conduct the Junk Fees Rule was written to stop.
  • Running a review-incentive program that doesn't disclose the exchange ("leave a review, get a discount on your next trip") — the Consumer Review Rule requires that connection to be disclosed, not just the review itself to be genuine.
  • Hiding or down-ranking negative reviews while surfacing only positive ones — explicitly banned as review suppression under the same rule, regardless of whether every individual review shown is authentic.
  • Sending a creator on a comped famtrip without a disclosure requirement in the campaign brief — §255.5 applies the moment a typical viewer wouldn't otherwise assume the trip was gifted, with or without an explicit posting obligation attached.
  • Treating "we don't control the hotel's fees" as a defense — the Junk Fees Rule's text reaches travel agents, resellers, and third-party platforms directly, not only the property setting the fee.

Where to actually start

In order: walk your own booking funnel end to end on both desktop and mobile and confirm the price shown in every ad, landing page, and checkout step matches what a traveler is actually charged, with every mandatory fee visible before payment; audit any review-incentive program for a disclosed exchange, and confirm no workflow is filtering out negative reviews before they publish; review every active or recent influencer, creator, and famtrip partnership for a clear, conspicuous disclosure standard written into the brief rather than left to the creator; then build out the local SEO, paid search, social, and content layers that grow any travel business, now that the pricing, reviews, and endorsement content feeding them are clean. The travel businesses handling this well right now aren't pulling back on aggressive pricing, review generation, or influencer partnerships — they're the ones that fixed the disclosure gap before a $35 million case, a warning letter, or a traveler's complaint made the decision for them.

Frequently asked questions

What is the FTC's Junk Fees Rule and does it apply to travel agencies and tour operators?

Yes. The FTC's Rule on Unfair or Deceptive Fees (16 CFR Part 464), effective May 12, 2025, covers any business that sells short-term lodging (hotels, vacation rentals) or live-event tickets to consumers, and its text explicitly reaches third-party platforms, resellers, and travel agents, not just the property or venue itself. It requires the total price, including all mandatory fees, to be displayed clearly and prominently before a consumer is asked to pay, and it separately bans misrepresenting the nature or amount of any fee in an ad or offer. Civil penalties can run up to roughly $51,000 per violation.

What did the FTC actually allege in its case against the travel app Hopper?

The FTC's complaint, announced July 2, 2026, alleges that Hopper advertised a "no hidden fees" promise while charging consumers "Tip" and VIP Support fees that were pre-selected and not clearly disclosed, with one fee reportedly visible only after scrolling. The FTC also alleged Hopper overstated the benefits of its paid VIP Support and Price Freeze products, leaving many paying customers unable to reach an agent or facing long delays. The company agreed to pay $35 million in consumer redress and is now barred from misrepresenting fees, with a requirement to clearly disclose all charges and the total price before purchase.

Can a travel business still ask happy customers to leave reviews?

Yes — asking real customers for honest reviews is unaffected. What the FTC's Rule on the Use of Consumer Reviews and Testimonials (effective October 21, 2024) prohibits is creating, buying, selling or distributing reviews from people who don't exist or didn't use the product, including AI-generated reviews; offering or soliciting reviews in exchange for compensation when that connection isn't disclosed; a business writing reviews of its own service under a fake identity; and suppressing or hiding genuine negative reviews while displaying only positive ones. A travel business can run a review-request campaign and respond to negative reviews publicly — it just can't manufacture, buy or selectively hide them.
