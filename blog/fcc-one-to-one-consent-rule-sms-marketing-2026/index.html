The FCC's One to One SMS Consent Rule Is Dead. What Replaced It Isn't.

The FCC finalized a rule requiring each text and call marketing consent to name one specific seller, closing a loophole where one checkbox covered dozens of lead buyers. It was set to take effect January 27, 2025, got postponed, then was challenged in the 11th Circuit and vacated. The regulation is gone. The compliance expectation it created mostly is not, because carriers and SMS platforms kept enforcing it anyway.

K
Kryvex Media Team
Nov 13, 2026 · 9 min read

If your business buys or shares SMS marketing leads, or runs a shared opt in form that feeds several different sellers off one checkbox, the regulatory status of that practice changed twice in 2025 and is worth getting straight before you build another campaign on it. The rule in question is the FCC's one to one consent requirement under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act, and its path from finalized regulation to vacated rule to carrier-enforced norm anyway is the kind of story that's easy to misread as "nothing to worry about" when the real answer is closer to "the paperwork changed, the practical bar mostly didn't."

What the rule actually required

The FCC's one to one consent rule targeted a specific, well-documented practice in the lead generation industry: a single consent checkbox, often buried at the bottom of an unrelated form, that technically authorized dozens of unrelated sellers to text or call the person who checked it. The rule required each marketing text or call consent to clearly and conspicuously identify one specific seller. A consumer agreeing to hear from "Company A" could no longer be treated as having also agreed to hear from Companies B through Z just because a vendor bundled them into the same consent event. It closed what the lead generation industry itself often called the comparison shopping loophole, where a single form submission triggered a flood of calls and texts from businesses the consumer never knowingly agreed to hear from.

Finalized, then postponed, then overturned

The rule's original effective date was January 27, 2025. It never actually arrived on schedule. In January 2025, the FCC itself postponed the effective date pending judicial review, stating a new date would be set no later than January 26, 2026. That judicial review came from a challenge filed in the 11th Circuit by the Insurance Marketing Coalition, an industry group representing exactly the kind of lead generation businesses the rule most directly affected. Per coverage from multiple law firms tracking the case, including Wiley Law and legal analysis republished via Mondaq and JD Supra, the court vacated the rule. One analysis describes it as "effectively vacated" as of early 2025. The net effect: a rule that was finalized, delayed on its own agency's initiative, then struck down in court before it ever took binding legal effect on anyone.

The underlying TCPA itself was never overturned. Only this specific FCC rule interpreting and tightening one to one consent requirements was vacated. Real consent to be contacted by a specific business is still legally required under the TCPA regardless of this rule's status, and TCPA lawsuits over unconsented marketing texts and calls remain a real, active area of litigation independent of this one regulation's fate.

Why the practical bar didn't drop with the rule

Here's the part that catches businesses off guard: a vacated federal rule doesn't automatically mean every downstream enforcement mechanism relaxes with it. Mobile carriers and SMS marketing platforms sit between a business and its customers' phones, and many of them built compliance review processes around the one to one consent standard while the rule was still active or pending. Those processes, largely run by the carriers and platforms as their own business policy rather than as a direct legal mandate, have in practice continued requiring a business to demonstrate that a consumer's consent names the specific sender, independent of whether the FCC's rule is currently enforceable. A business that assumes the vacated rule means it can go back to shared consent checkboxes may find its SMS platform or carrier relationship enforcing the same standard anyway, just without the rule's name attached to it.

What this means if you buy or share marketing leads

If your business relies on third party lead generation for SMS or call marketing, the honest reading of where things stand is this: the specific federal rule mandating one to one consent is not currently in force, but the underlying TCPA consent requirement is, and the practical compliance expectation from carriers and SMS platforms has not meaningfully loosened. Reverting to a shared consent checkbox model because the rule was vacated is a legally and operationally risky bet, not a green light. The safer, more durable approach is to keep treating every consent record as if it needs to name your specific business, because for carrier and platform compliance purposes, it effectively still does.

Why the lead generation industry fought this rule so hard

It's worth understanding why an industry group, rather than a single company, took this fight to the 11th Circuit. The comparison shopping and lead aggregation business model that the rule targeted depends structurally on one consent event generating contact rights for multiple buyers. A single form submission on a lead aggregator's site has historically been able to route the same consumer's information, and the same consent click, to several unrelated companies at once, each of which then markets to that person as though they individually agreed to hear from that specific business. The one to one consent rule would have made that model illegal as written, not just risky. That's an existential difference for a business model built around selling the same lead multiple times, which explains why the Insurance Marketing Coalition, representing exactly this kind of lead buyer and seller ecosystem, pursued the legal challenge rather than simply adjusting its consent language.

What to actually check right now

Audit your current opt in language. If any form your business uses for SMS or call marketing consent doesn't clearly and specifically name your business as the sender, that's a real compliance gap independent of this rule's current legal status.

Ask your lead sources directly how consent is captured. If a vendor can't show you exactly how a consumer consented to hear from your specific business, rather than a generic "marketing partners" disclosure, that's a liability you're inheriting, not avoiding.

Don't treat "the rule was vacated" as "the risk is gone." TCPA litigation over unconsented contact remains active, and carrier enforcement of one to one standards hasn't tracked the rule's legal status one to one either.

Watch for a replacement rule. The FCC's own postponement language anticipated setting a new effective date; a court vacating one version of a rule doesn't prevent the agency from issuing a revised one. This is a live regulatory area, not a closed one.

Frequently asked questions

Is the FCC's one to one consent rule for SMS marketing still in effect?

No, not as a binding federal regulation. The rule was challenged in the 11th Circuit by the Insurance Marketing Coalition and, per law firm coverage of the case, was vacated. It never actually took effect on its original January 27, 2025 date either, since the FCC itself postponed that date pending the judicial review that ultimately overturned the rule.

If the rule was overturned, does that mean one consent checkbox can cover multiple sellers again?

Legally, closer to yes than the rule intended, but practically, no, because the underlying TCPA still requires real consent and carriers have largely kept the one to one standard as their own policy. Major SMS platforms and carrier-side compliance reviews now commonly expect a consent record naming the specific seller sending each message, independent of whether the federal rule mandating that is currently in force.

What should a small business actually do about SMS marketing consent right now?

Keep operating as if the one to one standard applies, since the practical compliance bar from carriers and SMS platforms has not meaningfully loosened even though the specific FCC rule was vacated. Document exactly which seller a customer consented to receive messages from, keep that record, and do not rely on a single shared opt in checkbox covering several unrelated businesses or lead buyers.
